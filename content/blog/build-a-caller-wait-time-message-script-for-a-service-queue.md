A caller who waits without information assumes the line is broken. A short message can confirm the queue, set an honest expectation, and offer a way out. The message is part of the service, not a recording that plays before it. A good script reduces abandonment without pretending the wait is shorter than it is.

## Key takeaways

- Tell the caller the queue is working and give a range.
- Offer a callback before the caller becomes frustrated.
- Base the estimate on recent actual waits.
- Provide a separate path for urgent calls.

## Tell the caller where they are

A caller who hears nothing imagines the worst. A short message should confirm that the call is in the queue and give the current position or wait estimate. The message should play early enough that the caller can decide to stay. Silence is not a neutral choice; it is a reason to hang up.

Update the message when the queue changes significantly, rather than looping the same estimate for twenty minutes. A stale estimate erodes trust faster than no estimate. If the system cannot update in real time, use a range and say it is approximate. A honest range is better than a precise number that is wrong.

Keep the first message short, no more than a few sentences. The caller wants to know that the line is working and how long the wait may be. A long marketing message before the queue is a reason to leave. The useful information should come before the promotion.

## Offer a callback before frustration builds

Offer a callback while the caller is still calm, not only after a long wait. A message at the start can say that a callback is available and how to request it. The caller can then choose without feeling trapped. A choice reduces the sense of wasted time.

Explain what the callback will preserve, such as the position in the queue or the details already given. A caller who fears losing their place will stay on the line even when a callback would be better. The script should be clear about what happens next. A vague callback offer sounds like a brush-off.

Do not offer a callback window the team cannot meet. Use the same windows as the call-back policy so the promises stay consistent. If the queue is short, a callback may be slower than waiting. Say so honestly and let the caller decide. The goal is a good outcome, not a callback for its own sake.

## Set a realistic wait estimate

Base the estimate on the recent actual wait, not on a target. Use a rolling average and round it to a range of five or ten minutes. Update the range when it moves outside the band. A caller can forgive a longer wait that was described honestly. A short estimate that keeps moving is harder to forgive.

Say what the estimate includes, such as the time until a person answers rather than the time until the issue is resolved. A caller who expects a resolution in ten minutes may be disappointed by a ten-minute wait plus a long call. The script should separate the two expectations. Clear language prevents a second frustration.

Give the caller a way to leave the queue and keep the request. A voicemail or a web form can capture the details without holding the line. The message should say which option is fastest for the caller’s issue. A useful alternative is better than a longer wait.

## Handle the caller who cannot wait

Some callers cannot stay on the line, even with a short estimate. The script should offer a callback or a message without making the caller feel dismissed. Ask for the best time and confirm the number. A calm exit is better than an abandoned call.

If the caller is calling about an emergency, the wait message should not trap them in a queue. Provide a separate path for urgent issues and say what it is. The definition of urgent should match the business, not the queue design. A caller with a flood should not wait behind a billing question.

Record the calls that abandon the queue and the reason if the caller provides one. A pattern of abandonment at a certain wait length is a signal to add capacity or improve the callback offer. The record should include the time and the queue. A number without context is hard to act on.

## Review the message with real callers

Listen to a sample of calls and note where the caller hesitates or asks a question the message should have answered. A script that tests well in a meeting may confuse a real caller. The review should include the callback offer and the wait estimate. Small wording changes can have a large effect.

Check that the message is accessible to callers who are hard of hearing or who use a relay service. Provide a text alternative and a clear way to reach a person. The queue design should not assume every caller can use the phone in the same way. Accessibility is part of a fair service.

Review the script when the queue volume or the team capacity changes. A message that was accurate in a quiet month may be misleading in a busy one. Update the estimate method and the callback windows together. A short quarterly review keeps the message honest.

Keep the tone calm and impersonal rather than cheerful. A caller who is already frustrated may read a bright message as indifference. Use plain words and a steady pace, and avoid background music that makes the message harder to hear. A simple recording that respects the caller is more effective than a polished production.
