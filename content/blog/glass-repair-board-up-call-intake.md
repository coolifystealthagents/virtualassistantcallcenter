# Emergency glass and board-up calls: capture exposure, occupancy, and access facts

*October 2, 2026*

A broken pane can be a routine replacement request or an exposed storefront that cannot be secured overnight. The difference is not the caller's use of the word "emergency." It comes from observable details: where the opening is, whether glass remains loose, whether people are at risk, what the weather can reach, and who can admit a crew.

Reception should gather those facts and obtain dispatch acknowledgment. It should not tell anyone to handle shards, enter an unsafe area, or treat a submitted ticket as proof that a crew is on the way.

## Locate the damaged opening

Confirm the street address, business or building name, and safe callback number. Then locate the opening within the property. "Front door," "second-floor east window," and "loading-dock panel beside bay three" prepare dispatch better than "broken glass."

Ask what type of opening the caller sees: door, storefront, window, partition, display case, or another glazed area. Record approximate dimensions only if the caller already knows them or can estimate from a safe place. Do not ask someone to approach loose glass with a tape measure.

Useful intake details include:

- exact opening location;
- approximate size as reported;
- whether glass is cracked, missing, loose, or hanging;
- whether the frame or door appears affected;
- cause in the caller's own words;
- indoor or outdoor exposure;
- current occupancy and business hours;
- rain, wind, or temperature exposure reported by the caller;
- onsite contact and access method.

Photos may help the dispatcher, but only through the contractor's approved upload channel and only when the caller can take them safely.

## Ask about people before property

If the caller reports an injury, immediate danger, fire, active crime, or another trigger covered by the emergency script, follow that script first. The glass contractor's receptionist is not an emergency dispatcher and should not promise a police, fire, or medical response.

Do not ask staff to sweep glass, remove hanging pieces, force a damaged door, or stand near an unstable opening. Do not certify that an area is safe based on a phone description. A suitable boundary statement is simple: "I can record what you see and alert our dispatcher, but I cannot guide glass handling or assess the area by phone."

The contractor should place this boundary beside the intake prompts. Hiding it in a long policy document makes it less useful during an urgent call.

## Work through an after-hours storefront call

A restaurant manager calls after closing. The front-door glass is shattered, pieces remain in the frame, and the manager cannot secure the entrance. No injury is reported. Rain is forecast, and the manager can stay onsite for another hour.

Reception records the door location, remaining glass, unsecured condition, weather exposure, occupancy, onsite contact, and access window. The request enters the emergency board-up queue. The receptionist does not ask the manager to clear the frame or cover the opening. The dispatcher decides priority, crew assignment, and whether more information is needed.

The caller hears the actual state: "Your emergency board-up request has been sent to dispatch with the one-hour access window. A crew and arrival time are not yet confirmed." If dispatch accepts and releases the job, the next update can say so.

## Separate security facts from security secrets

An unsecured opening may affect alarms, keys, guards, or building access. Reception needs enough information to coordinate entry, but a general service ticket should not contain alarm codes, lockbox combinations, temporary passwords, or detailed security routines.

Record whether the property contact has notified the appropriate security role, not the sensitive content of that conversation. Put protected access instructions in the contractor's approved secure channel. Verify a caller according to account policy before disclosing existing ticket details.

If a door cannot latch, describe that observation. Do not promise that a board-up will make the building secure or satisfy an insurer, landlord, or authority. Those decisions belong to the appropriate property and service owners.

## Prepare dispatch for the site, not just the glass

Board-up work depends on practical access. Capture parking or loading restrictions, gate hours, stairs or elevator access, floor level, and whether an onsite representative must remain. Note any condition the caller reports that could affect approach, such as debris, water, or a blocked entrance. Do not assess whether the crew can safely proceed.

Ask whether another contractor or emergency service is already present. This prevents confused handoffs without assuming what that party has done. Preserve each observation with its source.

The [Ready.gov home-fire preparedness resources](https://www.ready.gov/home-fires) provide general property-emergency context for policy owners. They are not a board-up specification or case-specific safety assessment. The glass company should maintain its own approved call triggers, access rules, and escalation map.

## Require a positive dispatch handoff

An after-hours voicemail, email, or chat post is not acceptance. The service system should record when the request was created, which dispatcher received it, and whether that role accepted it. If acknowledgment does not occur within the approved interval, use the backup route.

Statuses should remain distinct. Submitted means the record exists. Accepted means dispatch owns it. Scheduled means a service window has been approved. Dispatched means the field response was released. Completed means the assigned work reached its defined finish state.

Preserve the caller's initial report when the crew later finds different damage. Add field findings rather than rewriting history.

## Test the calls that strain the script

Training should include cracked glass with no open exposure, a shattered door after hours, an upper-floor window, weather entering a building, an injury report, an active security incident, a caller with no authority to grant access, and a request where photos cannot be taken safely.

Review whether reception confirmed the location, described the opening, asked about people, avoided handling advice, protected security details, captured site access, and obtained dispatch acknowledgment. Ask field staff which missing facts caused delays or unsafe assumptions.

Repeated errors may point to the form. If injury information is buried below measurement questions, reorder it. If staff routinely promise arrival times, make status and ownership visible beside the closing script.

## Close with what is known now

A strong close reads back the property, damaged opening, exposure, onsite contact, and current ticket status. It does not speculate about safety, coverage, permanent repair, or arrival time.

That clarity matters most when a caller is standing in front of a damaged property. The call team cannot repair the opening, but it can prevent the report from losing its location, urgency, access window, or owner on the way to dispatch.
