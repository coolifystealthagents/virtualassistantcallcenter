# Building a caller FAQ that reduces repeat questions

## Start from real calls, not guesses

Pull a month of call notes and mark the questions that came up more than once. Group them by topic, such as hours, pricing, preparation, parking and cancellations. Count how often each question appears before deciding what to write. A question that feels obvious to the owner may be rare, while a small detail may dominate the queue.

Ask the handlers which answers they give most often and which ones they are unsure about. Their memory reveals gaps that a written log may miss. Add the exact words callers use, including the confusing ones. The FAQ should sound like the phone call, not like a policy manual.

## Write one answer per question

Each entry should answer a single question in plain language. Put the direct answer first, then the detail that prevents a follow-up. If the answer depends on the caller, state the condition clearly. A caller should not need to read three paragraphs to learn the office hours.

Avoid copying internal policy into the answer. Translate the rule into what the caller will experience. If a policy is too complicated to explain, it may need to change. The FAQ is a useful test of whether the process makes sense.

## Keep the answer short enough for the phone

A handler reads the answer aloud while the caller waits. Keep the spoken version to two or three sentences and put the longer detail in a note for the handler. A caller who asks a simple question should not hear a long list. The short version is the one that gets used.

Include a line that tells the handler when to stop and escalate. A question about a medical symptom or a legal deadline does not belong in a general FAQ. Mark those topics clearly and link them to the right owner. The FAQ should not encourage a handler to guess.

## Put the FAQ where the call is handled

The best FAQ is the one the handler can open without leaving the call screen. Put the entries beside the script or inside the knowledge panel. Use the same labels the handler sees in the call reasons. A separate document that requires three clicks will be ignored under pressure.

Keep the search terms simple and add the phrases callers use. Test the search with a new handler and watch where they hesitate. If they cannot find an answer in ten seconds, the structure needs work. Speed matters more than completeness during a call.

## Assign an owner and a review cycle

Give one person responsibility for the FAQ and a backup for absences. Review the entries every month against the latest call notes. Remove answers that no longer match the service and add new questions from the queue. A stale answer is worse than no answer because it sounds authoritative.

Record the date of the last review beside each entry. When a policy changes, update the affected entries before the change takes effect. Tell the handlers what changed and why. A short update note prevents the team from mixing old and new answers.

## Measure repeat questions, not just views

Track how often a topic appears in call notes before and after the FAQ changes. A drop in repeat questions is a useful sign, but it can also mean callers gave up. Read a sample of the calls to see whether the answer helped. The number alone cannot tell you.

Ask handlers which entries they still avoid using. An entry that is hard to say or sounds cold will not survive. Rewrite it with the handler who uses it. The best FAQ is shaped by the people who speak it every day.

## Keep a path for the question that is not listed

Some callers will ask something new, and the FAQ should make room for that. Give the handler a simple way to record the question and the answer they gave. Review those notes in the weekly meeting. A new pattern can become the next entry.

Do not force every call into an existing category. A caller with a mixed request may need a short conversation and a ticket. The FAQ supports the call, and it does not replace judgment. Leave that room in the process.

## Update the FAQ after a service change

A price change, a new location or a revised cancellation rule can make several answers wrong at once. Treat the change as a reason to review the affected entries before the announcement. Update the spoken version first, then the longer note. Tell the handlers which answers changed and when the new version starts.

Remove the old entry or mark it clearly as out of date. A handler who finds two answers will choose the one that sounds familiar. Keep the review note with the change record so a future update can see what was revised. The FAQ should follow the service, not lag behind it.

## Check whether the answer reached the caller

An answer that the handler reads but the caller does not understand has not solved the question. Listen to a sample of calls after an FAQ update. Check whether the caller accepted the answer or asked the same thing again in different words. The second question is the useful signal.

If callers keep asking, rewrite the answer with a concrete example. A short example often explains more than a policy sentence. Test the new version on a few calls before updating the whole team. The measure is whether the caller can act, not whether the handler finished the script.
