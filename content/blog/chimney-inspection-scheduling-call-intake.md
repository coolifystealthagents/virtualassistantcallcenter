# Chimney inspection calls: gather appliance and access facts without a remote safety verdict

*October 5, 2026*

Homeowners call chimney companies before a seasonal fire, after buying a house, during a renovation, or when they notice an odor, stain, sound, or visible change. The first call should prepare a qualified inspection. It should not end with a receptionist declaring the system safe or diagnosing the flue.

## Find out why the inspection is being requested

Ask what changed or what decision the caller needs to make. A routine annual visit, property transaction, new appliance, water entry, animal concern, chimney fire report, storm damage, and smoke entering a room each create different planning needs.

Confirm the service address, caller's relationship to the property, occupancy, callback number, and preferred dates. Record whether the caller is the owner, tenant, buyer, seller, property manager, or contractor. The company can then confirm who may authorize access and work.

If the caller reports active fire, smoke, carbon monoxide alarm, injury, or another trigger named in the provider's emergency script, follow that route before gathering routine scheduling details. Reception should not tell a caller to continue using an appliance or reenter a building.

## Identify every connected appliance

Ask what uses the chimney or vent: fireplace, wood stove, pellet appliance, furnace, boiler, water heater, or another unit. Record fuel type and location as the caller understands them. A house can have several flues, so "the chimney" may not identify the affected system.

Capture installation or replacement dates when known, recent service, and any prior inspection report. If a contractor changed an appliance or liner, ask for the contractor and job reference. Do not decide whether the installation meets a code or whether one report covers another flue.

The caller may describe soot, odor, staining, debris, cracking, loose masonry, unusual sound, draft change, or water. Preserve the description and timing. Avoid turning "dark mark above the fireplace" into a conclusion about smoke leakage or fire damage.

## Ask about use without giving use instructions

Record when the appliance was last used and whether the reported condition appeared during or after use. Ask whether the caller has stopped using it, but do not direct a restart, test fire, damper adjustment, cleaning attempt, or temporary repair.

Questions such as "Can we use it tonight?" belong with the inspector or another authorized technical owner. Reception can acknowledge the concern, identify the review path, and provide only exact company-approved safety language.

The [U.S. Fire Administration's home heating fire-safety resources](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prevent-fires/heating/) can inform a provider's public education and escalation policy. A general resource cannot supply a property-specific safety decision. The inspection company should approve the wording its call handlers use.

## Plan access from basement to roofline

An inspector may need access to the appliance, cleanout, attic, mechanical room, chimney exterior, and roof area. Ask which areas are available, whether occupants or animals will be present, and who controls locked spaces. Record parking and building rules for multi-unit properties.

Note roof height, steepness, known damage, overhead lines, locked gates, or restricted areas as reported. The field team decides whether conditions and equipment permit access. Reception should not assure a caller that roof work is possible from a phone description.

Weather can affect exterior work. Capture the caller's deadline and acceptable alternatives instead of guaranteeing that the entire inspection will occur in a specific weather window. If interior and exterior portions may differ, the scheduler should explain that after technical review.

## Keep inspection, cleaning, and repair distinct

A caller may request "a cleaning" when the decision needed is whether damage exists after an event. Another caller may need an inspection report for a sale but also wants masonry pricing. Create separate requests when ownership differs.

Do not promise that cleaning includes repair, that an inspection includes every test, or that a visit will produce a certification. Record what document or outcome the caller needs and who requested it. The service owner defines scope.

For an estimate, attach the appliance list, reason, areas, access, records, and deadline. Mark which facts are unverified. The caller should hear whether the next event is a scheduling confirmation, scope review, document request, or site visit.

## Give repeated concerns a history

Link prior calls about the same odor, leak, stain, or draft concern. Include dates, earlier dispositions, and whether promised follow-up occurred. A new ticket with no history can make a recurring problem look routine.

## Prepare for rental and multi-unit properties

When a tenant calls, record the unit, property-management contact, lease maintenance channel, and whether other units report a similar condition. Do not disclose another resident's call or assume one appliance shares a flue with another. The inspection owner must verify the building layout and authorization.

For condominium or cooperative properties, identify whether the appliance, flue, exterior masonry, and roof access fall under different owners. Reception can route those questions to the association and contractor contacts. It should not settle responsibility or delay an urgent report while parties debate who pays.

Document the contact who will be present and the person authorized to receive findings. This prevents a technician from arriving with access to a living unit but no permission for a locked mechanical or roof area.

Quality review should sample an ordinary inspection, reported chimney fire, home sale deadline, water complaint, multiple flues, inaccessible roof, and caller seeking permission to use an appliance. Check emergency routing, observation language, scope accuracy, and owner acceptance.

The final record should allow the inspector to identify the property, appliances, reported condition, timing, prior records, access limits, and caller's decision. That is a useful appointment intake without attempting the inspection remotely.
