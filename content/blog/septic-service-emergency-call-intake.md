## Record what the caller can observe

Start with the service address, caller relationship to the property, callback number, and where the problem appears. Record the caller's description of an alarm, slow drain, odor, surfacing water, or indoor backup. Do not convert an observation into a diagnosis.

## Ask about access before scheduling

Note gates, animals, parking limits, tenant coordination, and the location of the system if the caller knows it. Ask whether the property has recent service records. The technician can decide what equipment and inspection are needed.

## Draw a firm safety boundary

The call handler should not instruct anyone to open a tank, enter a contaminated area, handle sewage, or silence equipment. Use the company's approved emergency language when the caller reports immediate danger, exposure, or an unsafe property condition.

## Confirm the queue position, not an outcome

Read back the address and reported condition. State whether the request awaits dispatch review or has an accepted visit window. Weather, access, and prior emergency jobs can change a route, so do not invent an arrival time.
