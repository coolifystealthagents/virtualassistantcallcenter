## Capture the condition without diagnosing it

After a storm, several callers may describe the same symptom in different ways. Record the property address, callback number, whether the caller owns or manages the property, and what they can see from a safe location. Ask about active indoor water, fallen material, blocked access, or downed power lines.

Tell callers not to climb onto the roof for the intake. If there is an immediate safety threat, use the roofer's emergency direction. The assistant should not identify structural damage or tell someone that a roof is safe.

## Keep the queue honest

Separate a request received from an inspection scheduled. Give each call a timestamp and a status such as new, awaiting review, callback attempted, scheduled, or outside service area. Do not say a crew is on the way unless dispatch has assigned one.

Insurance questions also need a firm boundary. Record the insurer and claim number if the roofer requests them, but do not predict coverage or tell the caller what an insurer will pay. The contractor and insurer own those conversations.

## Find duplicate reports before they multiply

Storm calls often come from a tenant, owner, and property manager for the same address. Search by normalized address and link related callers to one incident while preserving each callback number. This keeps three messages from looking like three damaged buildings.

Review the oldest unacknowledged requests at set intervals. If capacity is full, use the business's approved notice and waitlist rule. Accurate limits preserve trust better than a vague promise made during a crowded hour.
