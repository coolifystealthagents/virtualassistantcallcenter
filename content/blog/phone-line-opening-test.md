## Test the customer path, not just the headset

An assistant can log in successfully and still be unable to serve the first caller. The public number may route to an old queue, the greeting may announce yesterday's hours, a transfer destination may be offline, or the CRM screen pop may load the wrong workspace. A short opening test catches these failures before they become customer conversations.

The test is not a full disaster-recovery exercise. It is a repeatable check of the path customers are expected to use during the coming coverage period. Define one owner, a small set of test calls, and a place to record exceptions. Keep test records clearly labeled so they do not contaminate customer counts or trigger real follow-up.

## Start outside the phone system

Place the first call from an ordinary external number. Internal extensions can bypass carrier routing, menus, hours rules, and geographic settings. Dial every public number that has material traffic, including campaign, location, and after-hours numbers when they follow different paths. Confirm the business identity before listening for finer details.

The tester should record the time, number dialed, route reached, and result. Do not use a real customer's phone or record. If caller ID affects routing, use an approved test identity and document that condition. A test that depends on one manager's personal mobile phone will be hard to repeat across shifts.

## Listen to the entire opening

Check the greeting word for word against the current approved version. Confirm business name, hours statement, holiday notice, language choices, accessibility option, and any recording or consent notice required by the business. The call team should not make legal conclusions about notices; the authorized owner supplies the wording and decides where it applies.

Let the message finish. Errors often sit after the familiar first sentence: a dead extension, outdated promotion, duplicate menu item, or instruction to visit a retired page. Note audio quality, but avoid subjective labels alone. "Greeting cuts off after seven seconds" gives the system owner something to investigate.

## Exercise one route and one fallback

Choose a common call reason and follow the same menu or routing step a customer would use. Confirm that the call reaches the correct queue, the assistant sees the expected number or label, and the approved caller record opens. Then test a fallback, such as no answer at a transfer destination or an unavailable primary owner.

Do not test every extension every morning if that delays opening. Rotate lower-volume paths on a schedule and test high-risk changes immediately after they are approved. The daily check should remain short enough that teams actually complete it. A deeper periodic test can cover every branch.

## Check the assistant's working tools

The answerer should open the current script, knowledge source, calendar, CRM queue, and escalation map before accepting calls. Verify access using a test record that contains no customer data. Confirm that the assistant can create, assign, and close a test task without triggering a live notification or form submission.

Login success is not the same as correct access. Check that the worker sees the right business, location, and queue, and cannot see areas outside the assigned role. If access is broader or narrower than expected, stop the affected lane and notify the tool owner. The assistant should not borrow another person's credentials to get through opening.

## Verify time-sensitive information

Hours, staff availability, appointment capacity, service areas, and on-call ownership can change daily. Compare the call-facing source with the approved operating calendar. The assistant should know which source controls when a website, chat message, and old note disagree. Do not reconcile conflicting sources by choosing the newest-looking one.

Pay special attention to time zones and temporary changes. A line may be open for the assistant while the destination office remains closed. The caller message and routing behavior should reflect the service actually available, not merely the phone team's login time.

## Keep test calls out of production work

Label the call as a test at the beginning and in every created record. Use a reserved test identity and disposition if available. Remove or archive test artifacts according to the business's approved procedure. Do not delete evidence that the system owner needs to diagnose a failure, but do not leave fake leads or appointments in customer queues.

Automations deserve attention. A test call can trigger confirmation texts, sales sequences, analytics, recordings, or manager alerts. Configure safe test suppression before the routine begins. The opening check must never submit a contact or booking form, create a billable order, or contact an uninvolved person.

## Create a stop, limit, or open decision

The result should lead to one of three decisions. "Open" means the tested path is ready. "Limited" means a documented subset can operate while an affected path stays closed. "Stop" means callers would receive an unsafe, materially false, or unusable experience. Define who may make each decision and who communicates the status to the call team.

An assistant may report the observed failure but should not reconfigure production routing unless that responsibility is explicitly assigned. The note should include the observation, test conditions, affected number, temporary instruction, owner, and next review time.

## Review repeated failures

Track exceptions by route and failure type. Repeated expired greetings may point to weak change ownership. Frequent access problems may reflect account provisioning or shift scheduling. A transfer that fails only before a destination opens may need a better hours rule. The opening log supports these investigations, but it does not prove a root cause by itself.

Retire checks that never influence an opening decision and add checks after a real gap appears. Keep the routine focused on customer-facing continuity. Long checklists invite box-ticking and delay the first useful call.

## Define the opening record

The daily test is complete when the public path was called externally, the approved greeting was heard, a common route and fallback worked, required tools opened in the correct workspace, and any exception has an owner. Record the coverage period and tester so the next shift knows what was actually checked.

A good opening test takes discipline more than technical expertise. It gives a virtual call team a shared answer to a simple question: if a customer calls now, will the call reach a prepared person with a working next step? Finding out before the customer does is the entire point.
