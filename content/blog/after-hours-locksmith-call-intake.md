## Start with location and immediate danger

A person locked outside a house at midnight wants a straight answer. The assistant can still be helpful without acting as the locksmith. Ask for the service address, a callback number, and a short description of the lock problem. Confirm whether a child, vulnerable adult, pet, running vehicle, fire, or medical concern is involved. If there is immediate danger, direct the caller to local emergency services rather than keeping them in a sales intake.

Do not ask the caller to prove ownership by sending an identity document to a shared inbox. The technician or business owner should set the identity check used at the property. The assistant only records the facts needed to route the request.

## Describe the next step accurately

Say that the on-call technician will review the request and contact the caller. Do not promise a price, a specific arrival time, or that a particular lock can be opened. Those answers depend on technician availability, travel, the hardware, and the business's approved pricing.

Read back the street number, unit, city, phone number, and safest callback method. A single wrong digit can send a technician across town or leave the caller waiting beside the wrong phone. Mark the time the request entered the queue and the time it was sent to the on-call contact.

## Close the loop with the on-call owner

Every after-hours queue needs a named owner and a backup. If the first contact does not acknowledge the request within the business's stated window, send it to the backup. The assistant should not invent a new escalation path during the call.

Log the outcome as acknowledged, declined, unreachable, or caller cancelled. Review missed and late acknowledgments the next morning. That small record shows whether the coverage plan worked without treating every unconfirmed request as a completed dispatch.
