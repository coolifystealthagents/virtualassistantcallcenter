## Separate discomfort from immediate risk

"The air is out" can describe a warm room, a failed commercial unit, or a household where heat creates a serious concern. Ask for the service address, callback number, when cooling stopped, and whether the caller notices smoke, sparking, or a burning smell. For immediate danger, use the HVAC company's emergency script, which may direct the caller to leave the area and contact emergency services.

The assistant should not diagnose a capacitor, refrigerant leak, or electrical fault. Diagnosis belongs to a qualified technician. Record the caller's own observations in plain language instead of translating them into a repair conclusion.

## Capture facts the dispatcher can use

Ask whether the system serves a home or business, whether all zones are affected, and whether a vulnerable occupant is present. Record the equipment type and brand only if the caller knows them. Do not make model numbers a condition for accepting the request.

Read back the address and phone number. If the caller is a tenant, record the property manager or owner contact according to the contractor's policy. Never tell a tenant that a repair has been authorized unless the approved record says so.

## Use priority labels with written definitions

Labels such as urgent and routine are useful only when the HVAC company defines them. The assistant applies the written label and sends the intake to the dispatcher. The dispatcher decides routing, schedule, scope, and price.

At shift close, compare queued calls with acknowledgments. A no-cooling request that never reached the dispatcher is a handoff failure even if the intake note was perfect. Track that separately from calls the business could not accept because its schedule was full.
