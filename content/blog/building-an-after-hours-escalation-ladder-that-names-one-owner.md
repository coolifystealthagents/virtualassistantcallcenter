# Building an after-hours escalation ladder that names one owner

## Start with the calls that cannot wait

List the situations that genuinely need a person before the next business day. A burst pipe, a medical triage request or a security alarm usually belongs on that list. A routine appointment change usually does not. Sorting the real emergencies first keeps the ladder short and credible.

Ask the business owner to defend each item on the list. If the answer is that a delay would cause harm, loss or a legal obligation, the item stays. If the answer is that the caller would prefer an answer now, it belongs in the next-day queue. A ladder that escalates everything is the same as no ladder.

## Name a primary and a backup

Every rung needs one primary contact and one named backup, with a phone number that reaches a person. A team email address is not a contact for an urgent call. The assistant should know the order of contact and how long to wait before moving down the ladder. Ambiguity at this point causes the most delay.

Update the roster whenever someone changes role, goes on leave or changes a number. Store the roster in the same place the assistant already uses during a call. Add the date of the last update so an old copy is easy to spot. An outdated ladder is more dangerous than a short one.

## Set a response clock for each rung

Give each rung a clear time limit, such as five minutes for the primary and ten for the backup. The clock should start when the assistant makes the first contact attempt. If nobody answers within the limit, the assistant moves to the next rung without asking for permission. The rule removes hesitation at the worst moment.

Record the time of each attempt and the result. A missed call, a voicemail and a text message are different outcomes and should be logged separately. When the ladder is exhausted, the assistant follows a final instruction, such as leaving a message and opening a priority ticket. The final step should never be undefined.

## Package the information before calling

An escalation call should begin with a compact summary that the receiver can act on. Include the caller's name, the number, the location, the issue and what has already been tried. Add the risk if the issue waits until morning. The receiver should not need to ask basic questions while the caller is waiting.

Prepare the summary template in advance and keep it beside the phone script. The assistant should read the summary back to the caller before ending the call. That step catches a wrong address or a mistyped number. A clear package also gives the next person a record to work from.

## Write the trigger for each rung

A trigger describes the condition that moves the call up the ladder. Examples include a safety risk, a total service outage, a caller who reports a break-in or a deadline that expires tonight. The trigger should use facts that the assistant can confirm during the call. Vague words such as urgent or serious invite inconsistent decisions.

Test the triggers against realistic calls. Ask the assistant to explain which rung a sample call would reach and why. Compare the answers with what the business owner intended. Where the answers differ, rewrite the trigger instead of correcting the person after the fact.

## Rehearse the ladder without a live crisis

Run a short drill once a quarter with the primary, the backup and the assistant. Use a made-up scenario that requires two contacts and a final ticket. Time each step and note where the call stalled. The drill exposes gaps that a written plan hides.

Keep the drill small and psychological safety high. The goal is to find a broken number or an unclear instruction, not to grade the assistant. After the drill, fix one or two items and update the roster. Share the changes with everyone who might receive an escalation.

## Review the outcomes each month

Collect the escalations from the month and read the timeline for each one. Look for the time from the first attempt to the acknowledgment and the time from acknowledgment to action. A fast acknowledgment with a slow action still leaves the caller exposed. Review both parts.

Ask the receiver whether the summary contained what they needed. Ask the assistant whether any rung was hard to reach or unclear. Use the answers to remove a step or add a contact. The ladder should get shorter and clearer as the team learns from real calls.

## Handle a roster change without breaking the ladder

A promotion, resignation or extended leave can leave a rung pointing at someone who no longer answers. Treat every roster change as a reason to review the ladder, not only the contact list. Confirm the new primary and backup, then send a short note to the assistant. The note should say which rung changed and when it takes effect.

Keep one current copy of the roster and archive the older versions. Do not let two documents circulate with different numbers. If a change happens during a holiday, test the new number before the next overnight shift. A quick test call prevents a silent gap.

## Check the ladder against the call record

The call record should show the time of each attempt, the rung reached and the response. Read a sample of escalations and compare the record with the notes from the receiver. A missing timestamp makes it impossible to see where the delay occurred. A record that only says escalated is not enough.

Look for calls that reached the final step and then stopped without a resolution. Those calls need a follow-up owner and a review the next morning. If the same final step keeps failing, the ladder needs another rung or a different instruction. The record should drive that decision, not memory.
