## Qualify the next conversation, not the entire event

The first venue call should answer a narrow question: is there enough alignment to schedule a useful next step? A receptionist can capture the event type, date flexibility, estimated attendance, spaces of interest, accessibility needs, and preferred contact details. They can check the approved availability view and book a site tour or sales call. Contract terms, exceptions, final pricing, alcohol rules, insurance interpretation, and capacity approval stay with venue management.

This boundary prevents a common failure: the caller hears an enthusiastic “yes” before the venue knows the room, layout, staffing, and timing requirements. A better response makes the uncertainty visible. “That date appears open in the inquiry calendar, and the sales manager confirms the space and terms” is accurate. “You have the ballroom” is not.

Separate inquiries from existing-event support. A vendor requesting loading access, a booked client changing a timeline, and a new prospect should not land in one undifferentiated queue.

## Ask for the facts that change fit

Begin with the event date and whether it is fixed. Then ask about event type, approximate attendance, start and end window, setup needs, and which venue or room the caller means. Capture a budget range only if the venue has approved that question and explains how it will be used. Do not pressure a caller to disclose more than the next step requires.

A useful inquiry record includes:

- Caller and organization, if relevant.
- Event type in the caller’s words.
- Preferred date plus acceptable alternatives.
- Estimated guest count and expected layout.
- Site tour or consultation preference.
- Accessibility, language, or communication request.
- Major vendor categories the caller expects to use.
- Source checked, result shown, and questions needing manager review.

The receptionist should label estimates as estimates. A projected attendance of 180 is not a guaranteed count, and a room’s published maximum may change with tables, stages, dance floors, or local requirements.

## Distinguish availability from a hold

Many venue systems have several states: open for inquiry, tentatively held, proposal sent, contracted, maintenance blocked, or unavailable. The call guide should define exactly what the receptionist may say for each one. A blank calendar cell must not be interpreted as confirmed availability unless that is how the venue has designed the system.

If the receptionist may place a tentative hold, specify the duration, required fields, release rule, and customer-facing wording. A hold should have an owner and expiration. It should never silently become permanent, and the caller should understand whether another inquiry can still be considered.

Record the date, time, and source of the check. When two callers inquire close together, the sales team can reconstruct what each person was told.

## Keep pricing and packages anchored to approved material

The receptionist may share a current published starting price, package list, or minimum only when the venue approves it. They should also explain what the figure does and does not include. Taxes, service charges, staffing, rentals, catering, overtime, security, insurance, and vendor fees may change the total.

Do not build a custom quote during intake or imply that a starting figure is the final price. If a caller asks for a discount, exception, or price match, record the request and route it. The manager should answer from the current pricing authority.

Retire old package sheets and note the effective date of temporary offers. If a social post conflicts with the current source, preserve the link or screenshot through the approved system and ask the sales owner to resolve it. The receptionist should not choose which version wins.

## Handle vendor and policy questions without guessing

Callers often ask about outside catering, alcohol service, amplified music, candles, animals, parking, security, décor installation, delivery windows, and cleanup. These can involve contracts, licenses, insurance, building rules, or local law. Use concise approved answers and send the current policy document when authorized.

Route any question that depends on the specific event. For example, an outside vendor may generally be allowed but still need approval, insurance evidence, and a scheduled load-in. Saying “outside vendors are allowed” without those conditions creates a misleading promise.

The handoff should flag decisions that affect feasibility. If a caller describes open flame, late-night amplified music, unusually heavy equipment, a ticketed public event, or a large accessibility requirement, assign the question before confirming a tour as if the fit were routine.

## Make site tours useful

Before booking, confirm which space the caller wants to see, who will attend, whether the date is local time, and whether the visitor needs an accessible route or alternate meeting format. Send parking, entrance, check-in, rescheduling, and contact instructions. Keep those details in one approved template so outdated directions do not persist.

The calendar entry should include the inquiry record, not just a name and phone number. A sales manager preparing for a 250-person nonprofit gala needs different material from someone planning a 20-person workshop. The receptionist can make the meeting more productive without attempting to design the event.

If a tour is canceled, capture whether the caller wants to reschedule, pause, or close the inquiry. Avoid repeated follow-up after a stop request. For promotional calls or texts, follow the business’s approved consent process and applicable rules. The [Federal Trade Commission’s telemarketing guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/telemarketing) is one U.S. starting point; the venue should obtain advice for its locations and channels.

## Write a handoff the sales owner can accept

A good handoff contains the caller’s goal, event facts, source checked, actions taken, unresolved decisions, and promised response window. Assign it to a named owner or queue. “Sales will call” is incomplete unless the queue has an acknowledgment rule and backup.

For example: “Jordan is considering a 160-person awards dinner on 14 May, with 21 May as an alternative. Ballroom inquiry calendar checked at 14:05 local; both dates show open for inquiry, not held. Caller expects outside AV and asks about a raised stage. Site tour booked for Tuesday at 10:00; sales manager to confirm layout capacity and vendor requirements. Email confirmed by readback.”

That note preserves the limits of what was communicated and gives the manager a focused agenda.

## Review conversion quality, not only volume

Track inquiry completeness, correct calendar state, tour confirmation delivery, time to sales acceptance, unanswered-question age, and records reopened because important facts were missing. Compare the first call with the manager’s outcome. If many “qualified” inquiries fail on a predictable policy, add a neutral question to intake.

Do not make booking conversion the receptionist’s only target. That can encourage overpromising, hiding constraints, or scheduling tours that cannot succeed. Balance conversion with accurate fit, caller choice, clean handoffs, and stop-request compliance.

Review exceptions such as double-booking risk, outdated package language, accessibility requests, and failed confirmations. Calibrate reviewers on the same call so a score means the same thing across the team.

## Run a focused pilot

Start with one venue and a limited set of event types. Test a fixed date, a flexible date, an attendance estimate near capacity, an outside-vendor request, an accessibility request, a calendar outage, and a caller who does not want follow-up. Confirm that each path has an owner.

During the first week, review inquiries daily with the sales manager. Correct the form or source when mistakes repeat. Expand only after receptionists consistently distinguish inquiry, hold, proposal, and confirmed booking.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the receptionist confirm that a date is available?

Only using the exact status and wording authorized by the venue. An open inquiry date, a tentative hold, and a confirmed booking are different states.

### Should every inquiry receive a site tour?

Not necessarily. The venue should define minimum fit information and the approved next step. Some questions need manager review before a tour is useful.

### How much pricing can be shared?

Share only current approved material and its limitations. Custom quotes, discounts, contract interpretation, and exceptions belong with the authorized sales owner.
