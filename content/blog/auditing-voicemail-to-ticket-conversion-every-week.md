# Auditing voicemail-to-ticket conversion every week

## Decide what conversion means

A voicemail converts when a named person accepts ownership and a ticket records the request. The ticket must carry the caller, the reason, the promised response window and the next action. A message that was listened to but not assigned has not converted. Write that definition down before anyone counts anything.

The definition should separate a service request from a sales pitch or a wrong number. A solicitation may be discarded, but the decision still needs a reason in the log. A caller who leaves two messages about one issue should produce one ticket with a link to both recordings. Clear rules keep the count honest.

## Map the path from inbox to ticket

Walk through the actual path a message takes before you build the report. Note who listens, where the recording lives, which system creates the ticket and who assigns it. Ask the person who does the work to show you the screen. A written process that nobody follows will mislead the audit.

Look for manual steps that depend on one person's memory. A message that waits for a supervisor to return is a system gap, not a performance problem. Record the average wait at each handoff and the point where messages most often stop. The map becomes the backbone of the weekly review.

## Draw the sample from the same window

Choose a fixed window, such as Monday through Sunday, and count every message that arrived inside it. Do not sample only the messages that already have tickets, because that hides the failures. Pull the list from the phone system rather than from the ticket queue. The two sources should agree on the total.

If the volumes are large, review a random sample and state the sample size. A random draw is more trustworthy than the first twenty messages in the list. Keep the message identifiers so another person can repeat the check. The same method each week makes the trend meaningful.

## Separate technical failures from caller choices

Some messages never convert because the recording is silent, the caller hung up or the number is invalid. Those are technical or caller-driven outcomes, and they belong in their own category. Do not count them as staff failures without reading the recording. The distinction prevents the audit from blaming the wrong step.

Other callers leave a message and then call again before anyone responds. That repeat contact is a signal about the promise, not proof that the first message was ignored. Compare the timing of the second call with the first ticket action. The pattern often points to a response window that is too long.

## Read the quality of the ticket

A ticket can exist and still fail the caller. Read a small sample for a clear summary, the callback number, the reason and the owner. Check whether the note repeats the recording word for word or captures the actual request. A short, accurate summary is more useful than a transcript.

Look for promises that the team cannot keep, such as a same-hour callback during a night shift. Compare the promised window with the staffing on that shift. A promise that ignores capacity creates late callbacks and repeat calls. The audit should flag those promises for a process fix, not just a note to the agent.

## Assign one corrective action per finding

End each weekly review with a small number of specific actions and one owner for each. An action such as "update the after-hours script" is testable, while "improve conversion" is not. Give the action a due date and the evidence that will show it worked. Review the action at the next meeting.

Keep the list short enough to finish. Five open actions are easier to track than twenty, and a short list makes it obvious when something is stuck. If the same finding appears for three weeks, treat it as a design problem. Escalate it to the person who can change the queue or the system.

## Report the trend with its limits

Show the conversion rate, the unassigned count and the median time to first action beside the sample size. Add a short note about anything unusual, such as a holiday or a system outage. A single week cannot prove that a change worked, so compare at least four weeks. The direction matters more than one perfect number.

Do not present the conversion rate as a measure of caller satisfaction. It shows whether messages became owned work, and nothing more. Pair it with a read of the recordings and the repeat-contact pattern before drawing a conclusion. That combination gives the team a fair and useful picture.

## Test one change on a single queue

An audit can point to several fixes at once, and trying all of them together makes the next result hard to read. Pick one queue and one change, such as a mandatory owner field or a shorter callback promise. Run it for two weeks and compare the same measures. The other queues continue as before, which gives you a control group.

Tell the handlers what changed and why before the test starts. Ask them to report a problem with the change instead of working around it. At the end of the test, compare the conversion rate and the ticket quality with the earlier weeks. Keep the change only if the improvement is visible and the handlers still trust the process.

## Keep the audit separate from a scorecard

The audit is a management tool for finding broken handoffs, not a ranking of individual agents. A scorecard that ranks people on conversion can encourage them to close messages quickly instead of solving the request. Keep the two reports separate and use the audit to change the system. Use the scorecard for coaching only after the system works.

Share the audit result with the team in a form that invites questions. The handlers often know why a message stalled before the report does. Ask them which step feels unclear or which owner is hard to reach. Their answer may save a week of analysis and produce a better fix.
