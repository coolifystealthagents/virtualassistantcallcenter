Peak season can double the call volume without doubling the team. An overflow plan helps a home services company answer the urgent calls first and treat the rest with a clear callback. The plan should be written before the rush, not improvised during it. A short card beside the phone is enough to keep the priorities visible.

## Key takeaways

- Rank call types by urgency before the season starts.
- Protect one line for emergencies and safety issues.
- Offer a callback with a real window, not a vague promise.
- Review the overflow data after the peak and adjust the plan.

## Rank call types before the rush

List the calls the company receives and rank them by urgency. A gas smell or a burst pipe belongs on the emergency line, while a quote request can wait for a callback. A shared ranking lets every receptionist make the same decision. It also prevents the loudest caller from setting the priority.

Share the ranking with the dispatch team and the owners so nobody is surprised by a change. If a call type moves up or down, update the script and tell the team. A ranking that lives only in a manager's head will not survive a busy Monday. A short card beside the phone is enough.

Include the after-hours calls in the ranking, not only the daytime calls. A no-heat call at night may be more urgent than a routine quote the next morning. The ranking should cover the full week. A separate after-hours list prevents a gap.

## Protect the emergency line

Keep at least one line or one queue for emergencies and safety issues. Do not let a routine quote request occupy the line while a customer reports a leak. If the team is small, route the emergency line to a mobile phone or an on-call dispatcher. The plan should name the person who answers when the main line is full.

Train the receptionist to recognize the words that signal an emergency, such as smell of gas, no heat, or water everywhere. The script should include a short safety instruction and an escalation number. Do not keep the caller on hold while looking for the number. A calm, direct response is part of the service.

Give the emergency line a backup number and a clear owner for each shift. If the first person does not answer, the caller should not be left with a ringing line. The backup should know the safety script. Test the routing before the season begins.

## Offer a callback with a real window

When the queue is full, offer a callback with a window the team can meet. A window of two hours is more useful than a promise to call soon. Record the caller's number and the best time to reach them. Confirm the number back to the caller before ending the call.

If the callback is likely to happen after hours, say so. A customer who expects a call at four and hears nothing will call again. The overflow plan should include a fallback message if the team falls behind. A short update is better than silence.

Record the callback window in the same system as the customer record. A window written on a paper list may not reach the person who calls. The dispatcher should be able to see the promise. A shared view prevents two people from calling the same customer.

## Keep the overflow queue visible

Give the overflow calls their own list with an owner and a due time. Do not hide them in the same queue as the emergency calls. A separate list lets the dispatcher see how many callbacks are waiting and who is behind. It also makes the backlog visible before it becomes a complaint.

Review the list at the start and end of each shift. If the same customer has called twice, treat it as a priority. A repeat call is a sign that the first promise did not hold. The receptionist should note the repeat and the original reference.

Set a rule for when a callback becomes urgent after a second missed attempt. A customer who has called twice should move up the list. The rule should be written so the team can apply it without a manager. A repeat call is a signal that the first plan did not work.

## Review the peak and adjust

After the peak, review the call volume, the wait times, and the callback completion rate. Look for the days and hours when the team fell behind. Ask the receptionists which calls took the longest and why. A short review turns a difficult season into a better plan for the next one.

Change one part of the plan at a time, such as the callback window or the emergency routing. Test the change during a busy week and compare the same measures. Do not redesign the whole process after one hard day. A plan that improves a little each season is more durable than a perfect plan that is never used.

Keep a short note of what changed after the peak review. A plan that is updated but not documented will be forgotten by the next season. Share the note with the team before the next rush. A small record is enough to preserve the lesson.

Keep the callback list separate from the dispatch board when the volume is high. A shared board can bury a callback under a repair ticket. A separate list with an owner keeps the promise visible. The two lists can be merged when the rush ends.

Keep a simple tally of how many callbacks were completed within the promised window. The number is more useful than a general sense of how the day went. Share it with the team at the end of the week. A visible tally helps the team protect the promise during the next rush.
