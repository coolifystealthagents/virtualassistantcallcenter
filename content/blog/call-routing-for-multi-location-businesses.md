## Resolve location before department

Use a location registry with branch name, service area, public number, timezone, hours, departments, languages, appointment system, overflow destination, temporary closure status, and registry owner.

## Collect only routing evidence

Do not ask callers to navigate the organization chart. Location, existing relationship, and requested service usually provide enough evidence to select the next queue.

## Maintain a location routing registry

Capture caller name, callback number, city or postal code, preferred branch, existing-customer branch, reason for call, service needed, appointment or order reference, language need, branch selected, rule used, local time, destination, and fallback result.

## Handle closures and ambiguous locations

When service areas overlap, offer the documented choice or send the case to the routing owner. Temporary closures use the declared alternate branch; unknown locations enter a central review queue; a failed branch transfer returns to the central fallback.

## Keep branches in control of exceptions

A central answerer may select a branch using approved geography and service rules. They may not claim inventory, local capacity, pricing, or appointment availability unless the branch source is current and authorized.

## Test calls that cross boundaries

Test a caller near a boundary, an existing customer who moved, a closed branch, and a service offered at only one site. Include calls placed outside the destination’s local hours.

## Find leakage between locations

Track first-route accuracy, branch corrections, transfers between branches, no-answer fallbacks, registry gaps, closure-rule use, and caller repeats. Review patterns by originating number and selected location.
