# Piano-moving estimate calls: document the route before anyone quotes the job

*October 6, 2026*

"It is an upright going across town" sounds like enough information until a crew reaches a narrow landing, a gravel approach, or a building that only allows moves during a two-hour window. A strong estimate call does not produce a price by intuition. It produces a route record that an estimator can review without making the customer repeat every detail.

Reception should gather what the customer knows, mark what still needs verification, and avoid promising feasibility, crew size, equipment, coverage, or price.

## Identify the instrument as the customer knows it

Ask whether the instrument is an upright, baby grand, grand, digital piano, or another type. Record the maker and model when available, plus approximate height or length. A customer may not know the category, so offer ordinary ways to find the information without turning the call into a test. A label visible above the keyboard or a prior invoice may help. "Unknown" is acceptable.

Ask whether any pieces are already detached, whether a bench or separate components must travel, and whether the customer reports visible damage. Copy those observations without judging the instrument's condition. If the move involves an antique, unusual finish, player mechanism, or other special feature, flag it for the estimator rather than declaring that standard handling will be adequate.

Do not coach the caller to tip, lift, open, dismantle, or measure the instrument from an unsafe position. Measurements taken from floor level can be useful when the caller is comfortable doing so. Otherwise, request a survey or approved photo review.

## Map both routes, not just the addresses

Capture the full origin and destination, the caller's role at each property, and the person who can authorize entry. Then walk through the path from the instrument to the truck and from the truck to its final room.

At the origin, ask which floor the piano is on, how many stair runs are involved, whether stairs turn, and whether a working elevator is available. Record doorways, tight corners, sloped walks, soft ground, loading docks, long carries, and parking limits as the caller reports them. Repeat the exercise for the destination. A simple ground-floor pickup can still end at a third-floor walk-up.

Avoid converting uncertain statements into measurements. "The doorway seems standard" should remain an unverified description. If clearance is close, the estimator needs actual dimensions, photos through the approved channel, or an onsite assessment.

Building rules matter as much as geometry. Ask about elevator reservations, certificates requested by property management, loading-hour limits, protective covering rules, and advance notice. Reception can collect the requirement and route it to the company's authorized office contact. It should not promise that a document or policy satisfies the building until that owner confirms it.

## Ask about timing in plain terms

Record the preferred move date, acceptable alternatives, time restrictions at each property, and any event driving the deadline. A real-estate closing, construction schedule, concert, estate handover, or elevator reservation may create a hard constraint. The reason helps an estimator understand the risk of a missed window without guaranteeing service.

Clarify whether another mover, storage facility, tuner, or property representative is involved. Get the relevant contact and handoff time if the customer is authorized to provide it. Do not arrange services outside the moving company's approved scope merely because the customer assumes they are included.

Weather can change an exterior route. Record exposed steps, steep drives, or an unpaved approach, but leave go-or-no-go decisions to operations. A receptionist cannot know from a phone description whether conditions permit safe handling on the scheduled day.

## Separate an estimate from an accepted booking

Callers often use "book" when they are still asking for a price. State which stage the record is in. An estimate request means the company needs to review the instrument, route, schedule, and service options. A quoted price is not necessarily a reserved crew. A deposit or signed authorization should be recorded only according to the provider's approved process.

The estimator owns the price and any assumptions attached to it. Reception should never fill a silence with a range copied from an unrelated move. If the customer asks whether stairs cost more or how many people will come, record the question and explain who will answer it.

The [Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's Protect Your Move resources](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move) give consumers background on interstate household-goods moves. The moving company must decide which rules apply to its service and turn them into its own quoting, documentation, and customer-service procedures. A receptionist should not interpret carrier obligations during an estimate call.

## Treat custody and existing damage carefully

Ask who owns or controls the piano and who may authorize pickup and delivery. An estate, school, church, storage site, or resale transaction can involve several parties. If authority is uncertain, preserve the request but hold the booking for review.

Record existing scratches, loose pieces, water exposure, or other reported conditions in the customer's words. Direct photos and documents to the secure approved channel. Do not assure the caller that a condition is cosmetic, repairable, or covered.

At handoff, the estimator should see which facts are customer-reported, which were verified from company records, which need a survey, and which decision is pending. That distinction protects the customer as well as the crew. Nobody benefits when a guess made during the first call becomes an apparent promise.

## Test the form against awkward jobs

Review more than ordinary ground-floor moves. Use training examples with a switchback staircase, a loading dock that requires advance booking, a grand piano with an unknown model, a destination still under construction, and a customer who is not the property owner. Include at least one call where reception must say that an estimator needs more information.

Score whether the agent covered both routes, read back addresses and dates, marked uncertainty, kept protected access details out of general notes, and described the next step accurately. If crews repeatedly discover the same missing fact onsite, fix the intake form before blaming call handlers.

A useful close sounds modest because it is accurate: the company has received the estimate request, the route details are attached, and the estimator will confirm what else is needed. That statement gives the customer a real next step without inventing a price or pretending the move has already been approved.
