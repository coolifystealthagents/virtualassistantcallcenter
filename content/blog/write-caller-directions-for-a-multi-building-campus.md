A caller who is already driving does not need a map read aloud. They need one landmark, one turn, and one place to park. A campus with several buildings makes that harder because the address may be correct while the destination is still unclear. A written directions script gives every agent the same short sequence and reduces wrong-desk arrivals.

## Key takeaways

- Ask where the caller is coming from and whether they are driving or walking.
- Give one landmark and one turn at a time, then pause for confirmation.
- Name the building, entrance, and reception desk in that order.
- Send a written version when the caller is driving alone.
- Review directions after any construction, closure, or signage change.

## Ask for the starting point first

Begin by asking for the caller's current location or the road they are approaching from. Directions from the north side of campus may be completely different from directions from the south. Do not assume the caller is using the main entrance or the address on the website. A short question prevents a long correction later.

Ask whether the caller is driving, walking, or using public transport. Each mode needs different details about parking, crosswalks, and accessible entrances. A driver needs to know where to turn before the building, while a pedestrian needs the path from the stop to the door. Record the mode in case a later agent follows up.

Confirm the appointment or service location before giving directions. The caller may have the right campus but the wrong building. Check the department, the suite, or the staff member's name against the booking. If the location is not confirmed, solve that first.

Repeat the starting point and the destination back to the caller. This gives them a chance to correct a wrong assumption before you spend time on turns. It also makes the call note accurate if someone else has to send the directions later. A thirty-second check can prevent a missed appointment.

## Give one segment at a time

Use a small number of segments: approach, turn, park, and enter. Give the first segment and then stop. Ask the caller to confirm that they are at the landmark before continuing. A caller who is driving cannot hold six instructions in memory while watching traffic.

Name roads and landmarks that are visible from the car, not internal building names the caller has never seen. "Turn right after the blue water tower" is easier than "turn right at the facilities access road." If the landmark is temporary or easy to miss, say what comes before and after it. Avoid describing distances in minutes because traffic changes. A visible landmark is more reliable than a street name the caller cannot see.

Say "left" and "right" from the driver's perspective, not from a map orientation. Use the word "turn" only when a real turn is required. If the route continues straight through an intersection, say that clearly. A caller who hears too many turns may take an early exit.

Pause after each segment and invite a question. Silence may mean the caller is concentrating, so give them a moment before adding more detail. If the caller sounds rushed, offer to send the written directions instead of continuing. The goal is arrival, not a complete recital.

## Name the building, entrance, and desk

Once the caller is on campus, name the building exactly as it appears on the sign. Then name the entrance they should use, especially if the building has several doors. Finally, name the reception desk or suite where they should check in. This order matches what the caller will see as they approach.

Describe the entrance by a visible feature, not only by a letter or number. "The glass door beside the flagpole" is more useful than "Entrance B" if the sign is small. If the entrance is locked or staffed only during certain hours, say so before the caller parks. A locked door turns a good route into a failed visit.

Mention where to park and whether the lot has a time limit or a payment machine. A caller who has to circle the block may arrive late even with perfect turn-by-turn directions. If visitor parking is inside a gate, explain how to enter and what to tell the attendant. Keep the parking instruction separate from the driving instruction so it is not lost.

End with the check-in step. Tell the caller what to say or who to ask for at the desk. If the desk closes for lunch, give the alternate contact. A directions call should end with the caller knowing the next physical action, not just the street address.

## Send a written version when the call is complex

Offer a text or email summary when the route has more than two turns or the caller is driving alone. The written version should repeat the same landmarks and entrance, not a different route. Ask which channel the caller prefers and confirm the number or address. Do not send to a number the caller has not verified.

Keep the written message short enough to read at a stoplight. Use numbered lines for the approach, the turn, the parking area, and the desk. Add the building name and the appointment time at the top. Do not include account details or the reason for the visit.

If the caller cannot receive a message, stay on the line until they reach a safe stopping point. Do not ask them to write while driving. Offer to call back in a few minutes if they need to park first. The agent should record the callback time and keep the line free.

Use the same directions text for every agent and every channel. A website page, a text template, and a phone script that disagree will create confusion. Store the approved version in one place and update it when the campus changes. ## Maintain the directions after a change

Assign one owner for the campus directions and a review date. Construction, a new gate, or a changed reception desk can make an old route wrong overnight. The owner should walk the route or check it with facilities before the busy season. A directions script is an operational document, not a one-time writing task.

Update every channel at the same time: the phone script, the text template, the website page, and the booking confirmation. A partial update is worse than no update because the caller may receive conflicting instructions. Record the change date and the version that agents should use. Tell the team before the next shift.

Add a short feedback field to the call note so agents can report a route that failed. A caller who says "I am at the wrong building" is giving useful evidence. Review those notes weekly and fix the landmark or entrance description. Do not wait for a formal complaint.

Test the directions with someone who has never visited the campus. Ask them to follow the written version and note where they hesitate. The driver's real experience is the best check. Repeat the test after any major change.
