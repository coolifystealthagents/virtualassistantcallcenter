After-hours property calls arrive when the office is closed and the resident is worried. The receptionist cannot fix the problem, but the script can decide who needs to act and how quickly. A good escalation path protects the resident, the property, and the on-call person. It should be short enough to use at midnight and specific enough to prevent a wrong transfer.

## Key takeaways

- Sort the call by safety and damage before transferring it.
- Call the named on-call person, then the backup, in order.
- Tell the resident who will call and when.
- Log the escalation and review the roster monthly.

## Sort the call before you transfer it

An after-hours property line receives a mix of emergencies, routine requests, and calls that can wait until morning. The first job is to sort the call before anyone is transferred. Ask what is happening, where it is happening, and whether anyone is in danger. The answer decides the path, not the tone of the caller.

Treat a fire, a gas smell, a flood, a lockout with a child inside, or a threat to safety as an emergency. A broken dishwasher or a dripping tap is usually a morning call unless it is causing active damage. Write the examples into the script so a new receptionist does not have to guess. A clear list is faster than a judgment call at midnight.

Ask whether the resident has already taken a safe action, such as turning off the water or leaving the building. Do not ask them to attempt a repair they are not qualified to make. The script should protect the caller first and the property second. A calm, specific question is more useful than a general instruction to stay safe.

## Reach the named on-call person

The script should name the current on-call person and the backup for each property or region. A generic emergency number that rings in an empty office is not an escalation path. Update the roster at a fixed time each week and confirm that the numbers work. A stale roster is worse than no roster because it creates false confidence.

Call the primary on-call person, then the backup, then the property manager in the agreed order. Do not skip a step because the first person sounds busy. Record the time of each attempt and the result. A short log shows whether the delay came from the roster or from the response.

Give the on-call person a short brief with the property, the issue, the resident’s name, and the safe action already taken. Do not forward a long transcript. The on-call person needs the facts to decide whether to attend. A brief that fits in one message is more likely to be read quickly.

## Tell the resident what happens next

Tell the resident who will call and when, even if the answer is that the on-call person will call within thirty minutes. A clear expectation is better than a vague promise to look into it. If the on-call person cannot attend, say what the next option is. The resident should not be left guessing after the call ends.

Give the resident a reference number and the main office hours for a follow-up. If the issue is not an emergency, tell them when the office will open and what to have ready. A short text message with the reference can confirm the call. The message should not promise a repair time that the team cannot meet.

If the resident is angry, acknowledge the frustration before explaining the process. Do not argue about whether the issue is an emergency. The receptionist can listen and still follow the script. A calm response often lowers the temperature enough to gather the facts.

## Document the escalation while it is fresh

Record the call, the issue, the people contacted, and the outcome before the next call arrives. A note written at the end of the shift will lose the times and the details. Use a fixed set of fields so the morning team can read the record quickly. The log is the handoff between the night and the day.

Mark the call as open until the on-call person confirms attendance or the resident confirms a safe resolution. A transfer is not a resolution. The open list should be reviewed at the start of the morning shift. An open item with no owner should be assigned before the review ends.

Keep a short note of what the resident was told so the morning team does not give a different answer. A mismatch between the night message and the morning message erodes trust. The note should be part of the same record, not a separate chat. A consistent story is easier to maintain than a collection of messages.

## Review the script after real calls

Review a sample of after-hours calls each month and look for delays in the roster, the brief, or the resident message. A repeated delay at the same step is a process problem, not a person problem. Fix the script or the roster before adding more training. The review should produce one change, not a longer document.

Ask the on-call people which parts of the brief were useful and which were missing. They see the property at the worst moment and know what they needed to hear. Their feedback is more valuable than a hypothetical review. A short debrief after a difficult night can improve the next one.

Test the roster and the script on a quiet week, not only during an emergency. Call the primary and the backup numbers to confirm they work. Walk through one scenario with a new receptionist. A script that is tested in calm conditions is more likely to hold under pressure.
