# A callback list cleanup routine for shared inboxes

## Define what belongs on the list

A callback list should contain requests that still need a human response. A message that was already answered, a duplicate of another request or a sales solicitation does not belong. Write the definition down and share it with the team. A clear rule makes the cleanup faster and less subjective.

Separate a service callback from a marketing follow-up. The two have different consent rules and different owners. Do not mix them in one queue because the list looks shorter. A clean list is organized by purpose, not by the convenience of a single view.

## Remove duplicates with care

Two messages from the same caller may describe the same request or two different issues. Compare the contact details, the time and the content before merging. Keep the earliest request and link the later one to it. Do not delete a message until the merged record shows what was combined.

If the caller used two channels, record both and choose one owner. A phone request and an email about the same appointment should not create two callbacks. The owner should confirm the request with the caller if the details conflict. A duplicate that is closed too quickly can hide a second issue.

## Age the list by the promised window

Sort the list by the time the caller was promised a response, not by the time the message arrived. A request from this morning may be more urgent than one from yesterday if the promise was shorter. Mark the items that are already late. The age should be visible to the whole shift.

Use a small number of age bands, such as due now, due today and due later. A long list with no age is hard to prioritize. The bands should match the team's service promise. Review the late items first and assign an owner to each one.

## Find the unowned requests

An unowned request is one that has no named person responsible for the next action. Scan the list for blank owners and assign them before the cleanup ends. A request that belongs to a team rather than a person is still unowned. Name the person who will check it.

If the owner is on leave, reassign the request to a backup with the context. Do not leave it in the original owner's name and hope. The backup should know the promise and the contact details. A short reassignment note prevents the request from disappearing during the absence.

## Close the completed items

A callback that was completed should be closed with the outcome and the date. Do not leave a completed item on the list because the note is incomplete. If the outcome is unknown, the item is not complete. The close should be visible to the person who made the promise.

If the caller did not answer, record the attempt and the next step. A single unanswered call does not always close the request. Follow the team's attempt rule and the consent. A clear close protects the caller from repeated calls and protects the team from an endless list.

## Handle the stale requests

A stale request is one where the window has passed and the caller has not been reached. Decide whether to make one more attempt, send a written note or close the request. Do not keep a stale item in the active list without a decision. The choice should be recorded with the reason.

If the request is old enough that the consent or the context may have changed, do not call without a fresh check. A stale callback can be more disruptive than a missed one. The team should have a rule for the maximum age and the approved action. The rule should be applied consistently.

## Assign the cleanup owner

Give one person responsibility for the weekly cleanup and a backup for absences. The owner should have the authority to merge duplicates and close completed items. The supervisor should review the stale decisions, not every small edit. A named owner makes the routine real.

The cleanup should take a fixed block of time, not an unstructured hour at the end of a busy day. Put it on the schedule and protect it. A routine that depends on spare time will be skipped when the queue is busy. The busiest weeks are when the list most needs attention.

## Review the list quality

After the cleanup, check the number of open items, the late items and the unowned items. Compare the count with the previous week and note the change. A shorter list is not always better if real requests were closed too early. Read a sample of the closes and confirm the outcome.

Share the result with the team and ask which rule caused confusion. Adjust the definition or the age bands rather than blaming the person who left a duplicate. The cleanup should make the inbox easier to trust. That trust is what keeps callers from calling back to check.

## Keep the list useful between cleanups

A weekly cleanup is not enough if the list is abandoned for six days. Add a short daily check to confirm that new requests have owners and promised windows. The daily check can take five minutes. It prevents the weekly cleanup from becoming a rescue operation.

Give the shift lead a simple view of the open, late and unowned items. A view that requires a report builder will not be used during a busy shift. Keep the fields visible and the list short. The team should be able to see what needs attention without leaving the queue.
