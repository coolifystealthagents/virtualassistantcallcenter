## Give donor callers a dependable service lane

Donor calls are not all fundraising calls. A supporter may need a receipt, want to correct a mailing address, ask about a recurring gift, report an unfamiliar charge, change a communication preference, or clarify a pledge. A receptionist can capture and route these requests consistently while finance and development staff retain decisions about refunds, tax treatment, restricted gifts, and record adjustments.

Start by separating service from solicitation. A person who calls to stop mail or recurring outreach should not be pushed into another appeal. A person reporting a disputed charge needs a prompt service path. The call guide should define what the receptionist may complete, what requires verification, and who owns each exception.

This structure protects the relationship. The caller hears what will happen next, and the nonprofit receives a record that can be reconciled against its donor system.

## Verify only as much as the request needs

Use the nonprofit’s approved method before discussing gift history or changing a record. A receipt resend may require matching name, contact destination, and gift reference. A request involving payment details or account ownership may require a stronger process. Do not ask for passwords, full card numbers, or authentication codes.

Record the verification result without copying reusable answers into free text. If verification fails, explain the safe callback path and assign the request to the authorized team. Do not reveal whether a named person has donated or how much they gave.

For memorial or tribute gifts, treat honoree and notification information carefully. Confirm what the donor wants communicated and through which approved channel. Avoid exposing the gift amount when the organization’s process does not include it.

## Classify the request before editing anything

Useful dispositions include receipt copy, contact correction, communication preference, recurring-gift question, pledge question, tribute notification, matching-gift documentation, suspected duplicate, disputed charge, refund request, and gift-restriction question.

The record should include:

- Verified caller and permitted donor record.
- Gift date or reference when available.
- Request stated in the caller’s words.
- Source checked and timestamp.
- Action completed under written authority.
- Finance or development decision still required.
- Owner, due time, and preferred response channel.

Do not merge two donor records because names look similar. Record-linkage decisions need evidence and an authorized owner. A spouse, assistant, or family member is not automatically allowed to change another person’s record.

## Handle receipts without making tax claims

A receptionist may resend an existing approved acknowledgment or receipt after verification. They should not decide whether a contribution is deductible, assign a value to donated goods, or alter goods-and-services language. Those questions belong with authorized finance staff and the organization’s advisers.

The [IRS charitable-contributions guidance](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/charitable-contributions) is a useful U.S. reference, but the nonprofit must approve its own receipt process. The receptionist’s script should say that the team can provide the organization’s record and route tax questions for appropriate review.

Track delivery. A message marked sent but returned as undeliverable is not complete. Reopen it, verify the destination through the approved method, and preserve the correction history.

## Treat recurring-gift changes as controlled requests

When a donor wants to pause, change, or stop a recurring gift, capture the request immediately and use the nonprofit’s authorized process. Do not make the donor defend the decision. State whether the receptionist has completed the change or merely submitted it for processing.

Dates matter. Read back the requested change, affected gift, and timing. If a transaction is already pending, explain only what the approved system shows and route refund or reversal questions to finance. Never promise that a bank charge will disappear by a particular time unless an authorized source supports that statement.

Keep payment credentials out of general notes. If new payment information is required, direct the donor to the approved secure channel.

## Record communication choices accurately

Supporters may want to stop postal mail, email, calls, texts, or all promotional contact while still receiving transactional records. Use separate preference fields rather than a vague “do not contact” note. Confirm the scope and effective path.

For outbound calling, the organization should follow applicable rules and its approved consent process. The [FTC telemarketing guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/telemarketing) offers a U.S. starting point. Record stop requests promptly and prevent a new campaign list from overwriting them.

If the caller requests an accessible format or different language, route that request to the designated owner and confirm the next communication method.

## Escalate sensitive gift questions

Restricted gifts, estate gifts, stock, donor-advised funds, grants, sponsorship benefits, refunds, charge disputes, and large record corrections deserve named owners. The receptionist captures the minimum facts and avoids interpreting agreements or financial consequences.

A useful handoff might read: “Verified donor asks whether a June gift was assigned to the scholarship fund and requests a corrected acknowledgment. CRM shows the gift and a general-fund code; no correction made. Finance review assigned, with email response requested.” The note identifies the discrepancy without declaring which record is right.

For an unfamiliar charge, record the date, amount shown, safe callback information, and any transaction reference visible in the approved system. Do not collect a full card number. Route it under the dispute process and state the acknowledgment window, not an unapproved refund outcome.

## Audit the full service loop

Review receipts, recurring-gift changes, preference updates, disputes, and failed verification. Check correct record selection, readback, action authority, ownership, delivery, and closure evidence. Useful measures include duplicate records created, preferences not propagated, receipts that bounced, changes completed after the requested date, and repeat calls before acknowledgment.

Do not use donation retention as the receptionist’s only quality measure. It can reward pressure when the caller wants a straightforward change. Accuracy, respect, privacy, and reliable follow-through are better service controls.

When similar errors recur, fix the form or integration. If preference changes live in two systems, define which one controls and how synchronization is verified.

## Pilot with realistic exceptions

Test a receipt resend, an address correction, a recurring-gift stop, a disputed charge, a tribute notification, a tax question, failed verification, and a donor who wants no promotional follow-up. Have finance and development leaders approve the boundaries.

Review the first week daily. Expand only after records reconcile and handoffs are accepted on time. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can the receptionist tell a donor that a gift is tax-deductible?

They should provide only approved receipt language and route tax-treatment questions to the organization’s authorized owner or the donor’s adviser.

### What should happen when a donor asks to stop a recurring gift?

Record and process the request through the authorized path immediately, read back its scope and timing, and distinguish completion from a pending finance action.

### Can a family member change a donor record?

Only when the nonprofit’s authorization process permits it. Knowing personal details does not automatically grant authority.
