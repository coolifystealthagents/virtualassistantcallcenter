## Define escalation by observable trigger

A practical matrix has rows for call types and three levels: routine owner, prompt supervisor review, and immediate safety or continuity response. Columns name the trigger, destination, channel, response expectation, fallback, and closure authority.

## Plan for unavailable leaders

If the listed owner does not acknowledge, use the timed fallback rather than sending repeated informal messages. An unlisted case goes to the duty manager; a system outage uses the continuity contact; imminent danger follows the approved emergency instruction.

## Build a three-level matrix

The escalation packet should contain call ID, caller and callback number, location, trigger observed, caller’s exact words when relevant, affected customer or service, action already taken, current risk, matrix level, owner notified, notification time, acknowledgement, and next checkpoint.

## Protect decision authority

An assistant may recognize a listed trigger and activate its path. They may not investigate beyond intake, decide legal liability, diagnose danger, promise a remedy, contact emergency services unless policy specifically directs it, or downgrade a level for convenience.

## Attach a minimum escalation packet

Use concrete triggers such as “caller reports smoke at an occupied site” or “service deadline is today.” Labels like serious, difficult, or VIP are too subjective to guide consistent action.

## Walk through edge cases

Simulate a routine complaint, a deadline-threatening interruption, an unlisted legal threat, and a safety statement while the primary owner is unavailable. Confirm the matrix never depends on one person.

## Review use and misuse

Track escalations by level, trigger accuracy, acknowledgement within the policy window, fallback activation, duplicate alerts, missing packets, downgrades, and closure confirmation. A rising count can reflect better recognition, not necessarily worse service.
