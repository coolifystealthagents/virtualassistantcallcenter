## Ask about the immediate conversation

When communication is difficult, ask which available language the caller would prefer for this conversation. Do not infer a preference from a name, accent, location, or the language used by a family member. A caller may understand several languages but prefer one for a detailed service discussion.

Explain the available options accurately. If immediate bilingual support is unavailable, offer the approved callback or interpreter path and state the expected timing. The assistant should not claim proficiency or translation support that the business has not verified.

## Separate preference from identity

The call note should record the operational request, such as "prefers Spanish for appointment calls," rather than a conclusion about ethnicity or nationality. Note whether the preference applies to all contact or only to a specific conversation. Give the caller a way to correct or update it.

Restrict the field to staff who need it for communication. Free-text notes invite inconsistent labels, so use approved language names and a separate field for the requested contact method. Never use language preference as a proxy for service eligibility or priority.

## Carry the preference through the handoff

Before routing, confirm that the receiving queue can meet the request. Include the preference in the visible handoff fields so the caller does not need to repeat it. If the next owner cannot provide the requested language, that owner should arrange the approved alternative rather than silently returning the case to a general queue.

Audit a small sample for respectful wording, correct routing, and stale preferences. Remove assumptions and correct labels when callers update them. The result should be a practical communication aid, not a permanent identity record.
