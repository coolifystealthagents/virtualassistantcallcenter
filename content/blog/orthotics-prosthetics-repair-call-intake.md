When someone calls a orthotics and prosthetics practice, the first conversation has two jobs: preserve the facts and move the request to the right owner. A call handler should not attempt the work of a technician, clinician, estimator, or emergency dispatcher. The handler should create a usable record, state what will happen next, and avoid a promise the operating team cannot keep.

This workflow is designed for a damaged, uncomfortable, or poorly fitting device and a repair or adjustment request. It can be adapted to the company’s service area, hours, customer records, and escalation table. The final script should always be approved by the business that owns the service.

## Define the outcome before writing the script

The goal is not to make every call short. It is to make every call complete enough for the next person to act without forcing the caller to start again. For this request, completion means capturing patient name under the practice policy, device type, affected side, visible issue in the caller’s words, ability to use the device, incident time, and callback number. The record should also show what the caller expects, what the assistant actually committed to, and which person or queue owns the next action.

Write the intake around decisions. A field belongs in the script when it changes routing, priority, preparation, or follow-up. Remove questions collected only because they have always been on a form. Long forms make assistants rush, and rushed calls produce vague notes. Put mandatory identity and location fields first, decision fields second, and helpful context last.

The opening can be simple: identify the business, give the assistant’s first name, and ask how the caller can be helped. If the caller begins with a long explanation, listen before forcing the form order. Summarize the request in neutral language, then fill gaps one at a time. That approach feels conversational while still producing structured data.

## Separate emergency recognition from diagnosis

The company should maintain a short, approved list of phrases or conditions that change the route. For this workflow, examples include a fall, open wound, severe pain, loss of circulation, sudden swelling, or another symptom covered by the practice emergency policy. The list should say exactly what the assistant does: transfer to a designated number, alert an on-call owner, read approved emergency language, or direct the caller to contact emergency services. It should also define what happens when a transfer is not answered.

Recognition is different from diagnosis. The assistant records what the caller sees, hears, smells, or experiences; the qualified owner interprets it. The script should never provide clinical advice, tell a patient to keep using a device, or decide whether symptoms require emergency care. If a caller asks, “Is this dangerous?” the response is not a guess. The assistant can say that they are not qualified to determine that, repeat the company’s approved safety direction, and connect the caller with the designated owner.

Keep the emergency branch visible at the top of the call guide. Do not bury it behind every routine question. Review the branch with the team whenever services, hours, or on-call contacts change. A stale escalation number is worse than a missing optional field because it creates false confidence.

## Capture facts in the caller’s language

Use observable descriptions. Record “caller says the display reads E17” rather than “controller failure.” Record “caller reports a strong odor near the rear unit” rather than deciding the cause. Put direct observations, caller assumptions, and staff conclusions in separate fields if the system supports them.

Read back names, addresses, phone numbers, and identifiers. For unfamiliar terms, ask the caller to spell them. When a number is important, repeat it in groups rather than racing through all digits. If the caller refuses a field, do not invent a placeholder that looks like confirmed information. Mark it as declined, unknown, or not available, then follow the company rule for whether the case can proceed.

Accessibility belongs in the standard workflow. Ask whether the caller needs a communication accommodation or a different contact channel when that affects the handoff. Calls placed through a relay service should be handled as real customer calls. The [ADA’s small-business primer](https://www.ada.gov/resources/title-iii-primer/) explains that effective communication depends on the nature and complexity of the interaction; an assistant should follow the company’s approved accommodation process rather than treating one method as suitable for everyone.

## Use a clear priority and ownership model

A practical queue can use three operational labels. “Immediate escalation” means the approved emergency or safety condition is present. “Time-sensitive review” means delay has a stated business consequence but no emergency trigger was reported. “Routine scheduling or follow-up” means the standard service window applies. These are routing labels, not technical findings.

Each label needs a named destination and a response target that the business can actually meet. “Urgent” without an owner is just decoration. The call record should show the queue, the assigned owner when known, the time sent, and the fallback if no one accepts it. For after-hours work, define who acknowledges the handoff and when the assistant stops attempting transfers.

Do not let the caller’s preferred deadline silently become a service promise. Record it as a preference or constraint. Say, “I have noted that timing for the scheduling team,” instead of, “Someone will be there by then.” If the business publishes response windows, use the exact approved wording and make clear whether the window is for a callback, appointment confirmation, or arrival.

## Walk through a realistic call

A prosthesis user reports a new crack in a cosmetic cover but no fall, pain, or skin issue. The assistant records the exact location described, whether the device can be used, the last visit, and the patient’s callback preference. A clinician or technician decides the next step.

A good note from that call would be concise but specific: request type, verified contact details, location, caller observations, relevant negative answers to the escalation screen, timing preference, attachments promised, and next owner. It would not include a diagnosis, a confident cause, or an arrival commitment.

Before ending, the assistant reads back the crucial details and states the next step. For example: “I have your request at the service address, the condition you described, and your callback number. I’m sending this to the review queue now. The scheduling team will confirm availability; I have not booked an arrival yet.” The exact language changes with the company process, but the distinction between logged, assigned, scheduled, and dispatched should remain clear.

## Design the handoff record

Create fields that another person can scan in under a minute:

- Caller and verified callback method
- Account, property, patient, project, or asset reference when applicable
- Service location and access notes
- Request category and plain-language description
- Time first noticed and current status
- Escalation questions asked and caller’s answers
- Photos, documents, or messages the caller will provide
- Desired timing, labeled as a preference
- Queue or person receiving the handoff
- Commitment made to the caller and next update channel

Limit sensitive data to what the business has approved. Do not place payment card details, passwords, security codes, medical narratives, or unrelated personal information in general notes. Access to recordings and records should match job duties, and retention should follow the company’s documented policy.

## Prepare exception paths

The normal script is only half the system. Decide what happens when the account cannot be found, the caller is not at the service location, an address is outside the service area, the requested time is unavailable, the on-call person does not answer, or the call disconnects during an escalation. Give the assistant a next action for each condition.

Duplicate calls also need a rule. Search by callback number, address, and open request before creating another case. If a matching case exists, append the new facts and notify the current owner unless the company process requires a separate incident. Never merge records solely because names look similar.

For an angry caller, acknowledge the impact without admitting an unverified failure. “I understand this is disrupting your day” is different from “We caused this.” Capture the remedy requested, preserve the caller’s wording, and route complaints to the authorized owner. The assistant should not negotiate refunds, liability, or contract terms without explicit authority.

## Train with examples and review the results

Practice one routine call, one incomplete-information call, one escalation, and one failed transfer. Score whether the assistant captured mandatory facts, used neutral language, followed the boundary, selected the correct queue, and explained the next step. A perfect greeting cannot compensate for a missing address or an unsupported promise.

Review a small sample of records every week during rollout. Look for repeated “unknown” fields, long free-text notes, wrong queue selections, and callbacks that lack owners. Those patterns usually show where the workflow is unclear. Change one field or decision at a time, publish the revision date, and tell assistants what changed.

Useful measures include record completeness, correct routing, handoff acceptance, time to first owner response, repeat-contact rate, and promises that had to be corrected. Do not reward speed alone. If shorter handling time produces more callbacks or incomplete notes, the process has shifted work rather than improved it.

## Use authoritative guidance without turning the assistant into an expert

The operating company remains responsible for its technical, clinical, safety, and regulatory process. Relevant background for this workflow includes [HHS guidance on the HIPAA minimum necessary standard](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/minimum-necessary-requirement/index.html). Use authoritative material to set policy with qualified advisors, then translate the approved policy into a narrow call-handling checklist. Do not ask an assistant to interpret a regulation during a live call.

Review source links and internal policies on a set cadence because guidance and business operations change. Date the script, name its owner, and keep a short change log. Retire obsolete copies so an after-hours assistant does not follow last year’s contacts or service boundaries.

## Put the workflow into operation

Start with ten recent calls in this category. Compare what callers actually reported with what the current form captured. Draft the smallest field set that supports routing and preparation, add the approved escalation branch, and test it with realistic variations. Confirm that every path ends with an owner and a caller-facing next step.
