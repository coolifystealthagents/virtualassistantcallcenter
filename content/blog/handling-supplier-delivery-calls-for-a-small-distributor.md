# Handling supplier delivery calls for a small distributor

## Decide which delivery calls belong on the main line

A small distributor often receives supplier calls on the same number customers use. That mix forces the person answering to switch between a sales conversation and a receiving problem within seconds. Decide which supplier calls should reach the main line and which should go to a receiving desk or a named buyer. Put the rule in the phone directory so a new handler can follow it without asking around.

Keep the main line open for time-sensitive delivery exceptions, such as a driver who is early, late, or at the wrong dock. Routine order confirmations can go to a shared inbox or a scheduled callback. The distinction protects customer calls from being stuck behind a receiving dispute. Write the rule down and review it when the delivery schedule changes.

## Collect the purchase order before the details

Ask for the purchase order number and the supplier name before discussing the delivery. Those two facts let the handler find the right record without guessing. If the caller does not have the number, ask for the invoice or the delivery date instead. Record the identifier exactly as the supplier states it.

A supplier may call about an order that was split across two shipments. The handler should check whether the purchase order has more than one open receiving line. Confirm the quantity already received before accepting a new promise. That check prevents a duplicate delivery from being booked as a new order.

## Separate a delivery exception from a scheduling request

A delivery exception is a problem with an order already in motion, such as a missing pallet or a damaged carton. A scheduling request is a change to when a future delivery should arrive. The two need different owners and different response times. Ask the caller which one they need before opening a ticket.

Delivery exceptions should go to the receiving lead with a timestamp and a photo request. Scheduling requests can wait for the buyer or the dispatch coordinator. Mixing the two creates tickets that nobody owns and callbacks that repeat the same questions. A clear label at intake saves time on both sides.

## Confirm quantities against the receiving record

Read the received quantity back to the caller before agreeing to any correction. The number on the packing slip and the number in the warehouse system can differ. Ask the handler to state the unit of measure as well, because cases and pallets are easy to confuse. A read-back catches the error while the supplier is still on the line.

If the quantities disagree, do not promise a replacement until someone checks the dock. Ask the caller to hold the delivery while the receiving lead counts the stock. Record the time of the count and the name of the person who checked it. The supplier needs that evidence to open a claim.

## Set a receiving window the driver can meet

A delivery window should reflect the hours when someone can actually open the dock and inspect the load. Do not promise a window that overlaps with lunch coverage or the busiest customer period. Offer two options and let the supplier choose. Confirm the chosen window back to the caller before ending the call.

The handler should also record whether the driver needs an appointment or a gate code. Small details like a locked entrance can turn a confirmed delivery into a failed one. Send the window and the access notes to the receiving lead immediately. A short written confirmation prevents the next call.

## Record damage evidence before promising a replacement

When a driver reports damage, ask for photos of the carton, the label, and the pallet before the load is moved. The evidence is easiest to collect at the dock. Write the claim reference on the receiving record and tell the supplier what happens next. Do not promise a credit or a replacement on the first call.

The receiving lead should decide whether the damaged goods can be used, returned, or scrapped. That decision belongs with the person who can see the stock and the contract terms. The handler can set a callback time for the decision instead of guessing. Clear ownership keeps the supplier informed without overpromising.

## Route credit and shortage claims to one owner

A credit claim and a shortage claim often arrive together, but they follow different paths. Name one person who owns supplier claims and one backup for that person. The handler should send both claims to that owner with the purchase order, photos, and receiving notes attached. A single owner prevents the supplier from calling three departments.

Track the age of each claim and review anything older than a set number of days. A claim that sits without a decision becomes a repeated call and a strained relationship. Give the owner authority to approve small corrections within a written limit. Larger claims can go to a manager with the evidence already assembled.

## Close with a written next step for both sides

End every supplier call with a short summary of what was agreed and who will do what next. Include the delivery window, the claim reference, and the name of the person who will call back. Send the summary to the supplier by email if the supplier uses email for confirmations. Keep the same summary on the receiving record.

The handler should also note what the supplier must provide, such as a revised packing slip or a photo of the replacement load. A next step with one owner on each side is easier to track than a general promise. Review a sample of these summaries each week for missing details. The habit turns a busy phone line into a reliable receiving process.

## Use a short checklist at the end of the call

A five-item checklist is enough for most supplier calls. Confirm the purchase order, the quantity, the delivery window, the evidence, and the owner. The handler reads each item back and marks it complete. If an item is missing, the call stays open or gets a scheduled follow-up.

The checklist should live beside the phone and inside the ticketing tool. New handlers learn it faster than a long policy document. Review the checklist after the first month and remove any item that does not prevent a real problem. A short list that people use beats a perfect list that they ignore.
