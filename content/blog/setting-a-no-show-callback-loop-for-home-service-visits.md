# Setting a no-show callback loop for home service visits

## Define a no-show before the crew leaves

A no-show is not the same as a late arrival. Set a waiting period, such as fifteen minutes past the window, before the technician calls the customer. The technician should document the arrival time, the attempts to reach the customer, and any access problem. That record decides whether the visit counts as a no-show or a delay.

Define the rule for a customer who is home but cannot answer the door. A locked gate or a barking dog can look like a no-show from the truck. The technician should call and wait a little longer before leaving. A fair rule protects both the customer and the crew.

## Call the customer while the technician is still nearby

The first callback should happen before the technician leaves the neighborhood. A short call can solve a simple problem such as a wrong gate code or a customer who lost track of time. If the customer answers, ask whether the technician can still complete the visit. Keep the call brief and practical.

When the customer does not answer, leave a specific message with the arrival time, the phone number, and the next available window. Do not sound accusatory, because the customer may have a legitimate reason. The message should make it easy to reschedule. A calm tone keeps the relationship intact.

## Give the customer one clear reschedule path

Offer a single phone number or link for rescheduling instead of several options. A customer who has to choose between a portal, an email, and a phone line often does nothing. The callback message should name the fastest path and the hours when someone answers. Make the path available in the customer's preferred language when possible.

The reschedule path should reach a person who can see the technician's calendar. A general inbox that nobody checks until the next day defeats the purpose. Set a response standard, such as same-day contact during business hours. Tell the customer what to expect so they do not call again.

## Record the outcome in the dispatch system

Every no-show attempt should produce a dated note with the time, the phone number used, and the result. A note that records the call time, the outcome, and whether a message was left is more useful than a vague no-show label. The dispatcher needs that detail to decide the next step. The note also protects the technician if the customer disputes the visit.

Use a small set of outcome codes, such as reached and rescheduled, reached and declined, or unreachable. Too many codes make the note hard to complete. Review the codes with the crew and remove any that nobody uses. Consistent notes turn a messy event into a pattern the team can study.

## Set the second attempt for a different time of day

A second call at the same hour will often reach the same voicemail. Schedule the next attempt for a different part of the day, such as early evening or the next morning. The customer's schedule may be the reason the first call failed. A little variation improves the chance of contact.

Limit the number of attempts before changing channels. Three calls across two days is usually enough before sending an email or a text with the reschedule link. Record each attempt so the next person does not repeat it. A clear limit prevents harassment and wasted labor.

## Coordinate the callback with the next route

A rescheduled visit should fit the route without pushing every later customer. The dispatcher should look at the technician's location and the promised windows before offering a time. If the route is full, offer the next realistic slot instead of squeezing in a visit that will run late. Honest timing prevents a second no-show.

When a customer reschedules, confirm the new window and the access details in writing. Send the same details to the technician and the dispatcher. A changed address or gate code should update both records. The goal is one visit that happens, not three visits that fail.

## Separate the fee decision from the callback

Some businesses charge a no-show fee and some do not. Whatever the policy, the person making the callback should not argue about the fee. Explain the policy in one sentence and route a dispute to the manager. The customer needs a reschedule more than a debate.

The fee record should include the reason for the missed visit and any evidence. A waived fee should have a named approver. Review fee waivers each month to see whether the policy is clear. A fair, consistent policy reduces friction on the next call.

## Review no-shows by route and by reason

Count no-shows by route, technician, day, and reason. A cluster on one route may point to unrealistic windows or poor access instructions. A cluster at one time of day may point to a scheduling conflict. The pattern is more useful than the total.

Share the review with the crew without turning it into a blame session. Ask what would have helped the technician reach the customer. Sometimes the fix is a clearer confirmation call, and sometimes it is a shorter route. Use the answers to change the process rather than the person.

## Close the loop after the rescheduled visit

After the rescheduled visit, send a short confirmation and ask whether the customer needs anything else. This step catches a customer who is still unhappy about the first miss. It also gives the team a chance to explain the process. A small follow-up can save a review.

Record whether the rescheduled visit happened and whether the customer stayed. Compare that outcome with visits that were never missed. The comparison shows whether the callback loop actually recovers customers. Keep the loop only if the evidence supports it.
