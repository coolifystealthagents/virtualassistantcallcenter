A small law office cannot afford to have its receptionist trapped by spam calls. It also cannot afford to mistake a real client for a robot. A triage rule gives the team a consistent way to screen without turning the phone into a maze. The rule should reduce noise and keep a human path open.

## Key takeaways

- Use at least two signals before ending a call.
- Keep a human path for any uncertain caller.
- Never confirm a client or case detail to an unverified caller.
- Review blocks and misclassifications every month.

## Recognize the common spam patterns

Spam calls often arrive in bursts and repeat the same opening line. A recorded voice, a long pause, or a request to press a number are common signs. The receptionist should note the pattern, not argue with the caller. A short list of signals makes the decision faster.

Some spam looks like a real client, such as a caller asking about a service or a document. The rule should not rely on one signal. Use at least two signs before ending a call, unless the call is clearly automated. A cautious second check protects a real client who sounds unusual.

Record the number and the pattern for repeat offenders. A number that calls every day at the same time is easy to block. Do not publish the blocklist outside the office. A short internal list is enough to reduce the volume.

## Keep a human path for uncertain calls

If the call might be a real client, transfer it to a person or take a message. The cost of a short message is lower than the cost of losing a client. The rule should say who makes the call when the receptionist is unsure. A second person can often hear the difference in a few seconds.

Do not use a challenge question that a genuine client cannot answer, such as an account number they have not received. A simpler question about the reason for the call is fairer. The goal is to filter automation, not to interrogate a person. A respectful question keeps the door open.

Train the team to end a spam call politely and without a long explanation. The receptionist should not feel obligated to stay on the line. A short phrase and a hang-up is enough. The rule should protect the receptionist from a caller who becomes abusive.

## Protect client information during screening

Do not confirm whether a named person works at the office or whether a case exists. A spam caller may use that information to build a credible story. The receptionist should take a message and route it. A neutral answer protects the client and the office.

Do not read a case number or a document detail to an unverified caller. Ask for a name and a callback number and let the appropriate person return the call. The screening rule should not become a verification shortcut. A real client will understand a call-back.

Keep the spam log free of client details. Record only the number, the time, and the pattern. A log that contains personal information creates a new privacy risk. The office should review the log and delete it on a set schedule.

## Use the phone system to support the rule

Use caller ID, a spam label, and a blocklist where the provider supports them. Do not rely on the label alone, because a real call can be mislabeled. Treat the label as one signal among several. The receptionist should still use judgment.

Set a short ring time and a clear after-hours message so a spam call does not tie up the line. A message that asks for a name and a reason is enough. Do not ask callers to press a long menu. A simple path is easier for a real client.

Review the blocked numbers each month and remove any number that a client says was blocked in error. A blocklist that is never reviewed can quietly cut off a real contact. Record the review date and the person who checked it. A short review is better than a permanent mistake.

## Review the rule after a real miss

If a real client is misclassified, review the call with the receptionist and the office manager. Ask which signal was misleading and what would have helped. Change the rule rather than blaming the person. A miss is a chance to make the screening more precise.

Track how many calls are blocked and how many are later confirmed as legitimate. A high block count with no misses may be working, while a low count with a miss needs attention. Report the two numbers together. A single count can make the rule look better or worse than it is.

Review the rule when the office changes its intake process or adds a new practice area. A new service may bring a different kind of caller. The old signals may no longer fit. A short update keeps the rule useful without turning it into a barrier.

Give the receptionist a way to flag a new pattern without waiting for the monthly review. A short note in the call log is enough to start a conversation. The office manager can decide whether to update the rule or the blocklist. A small feedback path keeps the rule current without turning every call into a policy debate.

Keep the receptionist script short enough to say while the caller is waiting. Two sentences that ask for a name and a reason are usually enough. A long challenge invites an argument and gives the spam caller more time. The script should sound like a normal business greeting, not an interrogation.
