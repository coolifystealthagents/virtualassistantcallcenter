## Headline finding

After-hours routing must answer two questions before a call is handled: what is the approved destination, and what happens when nobody accepts it?

## Methodology

Define hours, caller intent, urgent boundary, fallback destination, exclusion rule, and stopping condition. Compare ten public sources with test calls and local event records. Keep unanswered and ambiguous calls separate.

## Key stats and takeaways

- Two decisions determine whether an after-hours route is safe: destination and fallback.
- Ten sources can frame the test; local calls establish route reliability.
- Emergency language must follow owner-approved instructions and local requirements.

## Test table

| Scenario | Evidence | Review question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary | Correct queue | Did the route match intent? |
| Urgent | Approved escalation | Was the boundary clear? |
| No answer | Fallback event | Who owns recovery? |

## Operating controls

Publish a versioned schedule and route map. Use plain language, confirm the caller’s intent, and avoid diagnosing or promising response times. Escalate safety, clinical, legal, and financial matters.

## Quality review

Test every route after schedule changes. Check recording or note preferences, accessibility, read-back, fallback acceptance, and exception ownership.

## FAQ

### Is voicemail always an acceptable fallback?

Only if the owner has approved it for that intent and can review it.

### What if the caller reports danger?

Use approved emergency wording and escalate according to the owner’s policy; do not improvise.

### How should schedule changes be tracked?

Use an effective date, approver, version, and rollback path.
