# Testing whether callers recall consent language after a call ends

## Question and scope

This review asks whether callers can recall the consent language they heard during a call. The unit is one caller who completed a recorded or callback consent step. The observation window is a short follow-up within forty-eight hours. The review covers the caller's recollection of the purpose, the retention period and the right to decline.

The review excludes callers who were not asked for consent and callers who ended the call before the statement. It also excludes a legal determination about whether the consent was valid. The aim is to test comprehension and recall, not to replace a compliance review. Consumer guidance from the [FCC](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/telephone-consumer-protection-act-1991) and the [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security) provides context for the underlying obligations.

## Define what recall means

Recall is the caller's ability to restate the key points in their own words after the call. Recognition, such as agreeing with a statement, is weaker than unaided recall. The study should record both, because a caller may recognize a point without being able to produce it. The distinction matters when the statement is long.

The key points should be few and concrete. For a recording notice, the caller needs to know that the call is recorded, why and how to decline or ask questions. For a callback consent, the caller needs to know who will call, when and how to stop. A statement with ten points is unlikely to be recalled in full.

## Keep the statement short and specific

A short statement is easier to hear, repeat and remember. Remove legal language that the caller cannot use and keep the operational facts. Say who is recording, what the recording is used for and how long it is kept. If the caller must take an action to decline, explain that action plainly.

Test the statement with a few handlers before the study begins. Ask them to read it aloud at a normal pace and note where it feels rushed. A statement that sounds natural on paper may be hard to follow on a phone. The spoken version is the one that matters.

## Choose a follow-up method that does not pressure

The follow-up should be separate from the sales or service call. A short survey or a scripted call can work if the caller agrees to it. Do not use the same handler who delivered the consent statement, because the caller may give the answer they think is expected. A neutral wording reduces that pressure.

Record whether the caller declined the follow-up and why. A low response rate is a finding, not a reason to discard the study. Report the response rate beside the recall result. A result from a small self-selected group should be described as such.

## Score recall without leading the caller

Ask an open question first, such as what the caller remembers about the recording notice. Let them finish before offering any prompt. Then use a recognition question for each key point and record yes or no. Do not praise a correct answer or correct a wrong one during the call. A neutral tone keeps the next answer from being shaped by the last one.

Two reviewers should score a sample independently and compare. Where they disagree, refine the scoring rule rather than forcing agreement. The scoring should be simple enough to repeat. A complex rubric will not survive a busy week.

## Separate recall from understanding

A caller may repeat the words without understanding what they mean. Ask a follow-up question that applies the point, such as how the caller would stop future calls. A correct application is stronger evidence than a repeated phrase. The review should report recall and application separately.

A caller may also understand the statement and still forget it later. That is a normal limit of memory, not necessarily a failure of the script. The study should not treat every forgotten detail as a compliance breach. It should identify which points are consistently missed and whether the script can be clearer.

## Compare delivery methods

Different delivery methods may produce different recall. A live explanation, a recorded message and a written confirmation each have trade-offs. Compare them with the same key points and the same follow-up window. Do not change the order of the questions between groups.

Random assignment is better than letting each handler choose a method. If random assignment is not possible, report how the groups differed. A comparison without that context can reflect the callers rather than the method. The result should be presented with that caveat.

## Check the record after the call

The consent record should show what was said, when and by whom. A recording or a timestamped note is stronger than a checkbox with no detail. Sample the records for completeness and compare them with the call. A missing record makes a recall study harder to interpret.

The record also needs a retention and access rule. A consent record kept longer than necessary creates a privacy risk. Review who can open it and whether the caller can ask for a copy. The operational and privacy questions should be answered together.

## State the limits

The study cannot prove that a caller gave informed consent in a legal sense. It cannot measure every call or every language. It also cannot remove the effect of the follow-up itself, because asking about the statement may improve later recall. Those limits belong in the report.

The study can show which parts of a statement are consistently missed. That is useful for improving the script and the training. It should be presented as evidence for a clearer conversation, not as a compliance certificate. The team should act on the pattern and keep the record honest.

## A protocol for one queue

Choose a two-week window and one consent statement. Train the handlers to deliver it in the same way and to offer the follow-up at the same point. Record the calls and sample the consent records. Follow up with willing callers within forty-eight hours.

Score recall and application with two reviewers and report the response rate. Compare the results with the statement's key points and revise the wording. Repeat the study after the change. The protocol is small enough for a single queue and specific enough to produce a useful answer.

## Handle a caller who declines the follow-up

A caller may agree to the call but decline the follow-up. Record the decline and do not ask again during the same contact. The refusal is useful information about how the study feels to callers. It should be reported with the response rate.

Do not treat a refusal as a sign that the caller did not understand the consent statement. It may mean the caller is busy, wary or simply finished with the interaction. Keep the follow-up optional and short. A respectful process is more likely to produce honest answers from the people who do take part.

## Report the result

Report the number of callers invited, the number who completed the follow-up and the recall score for each key point. Separate unaided recall from recognition and from the application question. State the delivery method and the follow-up window. A reader should be able to see how the study was run.

Add the limits in plain language beside the result. A small sample and a short window cannot support a broad claim about every caller. The report should end with the specific wording change the team will test next. That next step turns the study into a practical improvement.

## Review the statement after the study

Use the points that callers missed most often to rewrite the statement. Shorten the sentence, move the key fact earlier and remove a detail that does not change the caller's choice. Test the new wording with a few handlers before the next study. The spoken version should sound like a person, not a form.

Keep the old statement and the new one in the same record. Compare the recall result after the change and report any difference. Repeat the study only when the team can act on the result. A study that does not lead to a change is just extra work for the callers.
