# Estimating call volume for a new service launch

## Question and scope

This review asks how a small team can estimate call volume for a service that has not launched yet. The unit is one inbound call about the new service during its first eight weeks. The estimate covers the expected count, the arrival pattern by hour and the share that needs a live person. The review is a planning method, not a forecast of revenue.

The review excludes calls about existing services unless the new launch changes their volume. It also excludes outbound sales calls and messages that arrive through chat or email. The scope stays on the phone demand that staffing must cover. Small-business planning context is published by the [SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/market-research-competitive-analysis) and labor context by the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/customer-service-representatives.htm).

## Start with a comparable service

Find an existing service that resembles the launch in audience, price and complexity. Use its first eight weeks of call data as the starting point. If the new service is broader, the comparison will understate demand. If it is narrower, the comparison may overstate it. Write down the differences before adjusting anything.

A comparable service does not need to be identical. The useful comparison is the shape of the demand and the share of callers who need help. A simple service with a clear price may generate fewer calls than a complex one with several options. The comparison should be chosen for those structural reasons.

## Build a range instead of one number

A single number invites false confidence. Build a low, middle and high estimate from the same assumptions. The low case might assume a slow launch and a strong self-service page. The high case might assume a promotion and a complex signup. The range helps the team plan coverage without pretending to know the future.

State the assumption behind each case in one sentence. A reader should be able to see why the cases differ. If the range is very wide, the team needs more information before committing to a large staffing block. A wide range is a finding, not a failure.

## Use the arrival pattern, not just the total

Two hundred calls in a week can be comfortable or impossible depending on when they arrive. Plot the comparison service's calls by hour and day. Look for a morning peak, a lunch dip and a Monday spike. The new service may share the pattern even if the total differs.

A launch promotion can create a short spike that a weekly average hides. Add a separate line for the first three days if the launch includes an announcement. Plan the first week with extra coverage and reduce it only after the actual pattern appears. The hourly shape is more useful for scheduling than the weekly total.

## Estimate the share that needs a person

Not every call requires a live conversation. Some callers need a status, a document or a simple answer that an FAQ can provide. Estimate the share that needs a person and the share that can be handled by a message or a self-service path. The staffing plan should follow the first share.

Use the comparison service's call reasons to make the split. If the new service has a more complex signup, raise the share that needs a person. If it has a clear FAQ, lower it. Record the reasoning so the team can revisit it after launch.

## Add handling time and shrinkage

A call count becomes a staffing need only when it is combined with handling time. Estimate the average talk time and the after-call work for the new service. Add an allowance for breaks, training and absences. The result is a rough number of productive hours, not a headcount.

Use a range for handling time as well, because a new service often takes longer at first. A new script and unfamiliar questions slow the first weeks. Plan for a temporary increase and a review after the team learns the call types. Do not lock the first estimate into a permanent schedule.

## Check the assumptions against early calls

The first week of real calls is the best test of the estimate. Compare the actual count, the arrival pattern and the handling time with each case. Note where the estimate was wrong and why. A miss caused by a promotion is different from a miss caused by a complex signup.

Do not change the estimate every day. Review it at the end of the first week and again at the end of the first month. Keep the original assumptions so the team can learn from them. A stable review cadence prevents panic scheduling.

## Decide how much capacity to reserve

Reserve capacity for the high case only if the cost of a missed call is high. For a routine service, a small overflow plan may be enough. For a service with safety or legal consequences, the high case deserves more coverage. The decision should reflect the risk, not only the average.

Write the trigger for adding capacity, such as a queue wait above a set threshold for two days. Give one person authority to act on the trigger. A plan without a trigger becomes a document that nobody uses. The capacity decision should be reviewable after the launch.

## State the limits

The estimate cannot know how the market will respond to the launch. It cannot separate the effect of a promotion from the effect of the service itself. It also cannot predict every caller's reason for calling. Those limits belong beside the numbers.

The estimate can still support a reasonable staffing decision. It gives the team a shared set of assumptions and a way to compare the actual result. That is more useful than a confident number with no visible reasoning. The report should invite correction, not defend the original guess.

## A protocol for one launch

Choose a comparable service and collect its first eight weeks of calls. Build low, middle and high cases with written assumptions. Plot the hourly pattern and estimate the share that needs a person. Add handling time and shrinkage to get productive hours.

Review the actuals after week one and week four. Keep the original assumptions and record where they missed. Use the result to adjust coverage and to improve the next estimate. The protocol turns a guess into a learning process.

## Plan the first week separately

The first week of a launch rarely looks like a steady month. Announcements, curiosity and early problems can produce a spike or a slow start. Plan that week with its own coverage and its own review. Do not use it to set the permanent schedule.

Give the team a simple way to record the call reason and the time. A short note is enough to see whether the launch created a new pattern or repeated an old one. Compare the first week with the comparable service after the spike settles. The difference tells you whether the estimate needs a new base.

## Report the estimate

Write the low, middle and high cases in one page with the assumptions beside each number. Show the expected hourly pattern and the share that needs a person. Add the handling time and the resulting productive hours. A reader should be able to trace how the estimate was built.

State what would change the answer, such as a larger promotion or a simpler signup. That statement is more useful than a false precision. Review the estimate after the launch and record the actual result. The next estimate will be better because the assumptions are visible.

## Avoid a permanent plan from early data

Early launch data can be noisy and flattering. A strong first week may reflect a promotion, and a weak first week may reflect a slow announcement. Wait for at least four weeks before changing a permanent schedule. Use temporary coverage to bridge the uncertainty.

Keep the original estimate and the actuals in the same file. When the team reviews staffing, it can see whether the gap came from volume, handling time or the arrival pattern. That view prevents a single week from becoming the new normal. The plan should learn from the launch without overreacting to it.
