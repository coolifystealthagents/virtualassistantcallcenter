## Why priority needs a definition

Property residents may describe a broken appliance, leak, lock problem, noise complaint, or access issue as urgent. Priority is not a tone judgment. It is a business-approved classification based on the reported condition, applicable property rule, and available response. A call record should not silently upgrade or downgrade a request because a caller sounded calm or upset.

This research design helps property managers study intake quality. It does not establish habitability, legal liability, lease rights, or repair diagnosis. Those matters belong to the property owner, manager, licensed contractor, or qualified authority.

## Cohort

Include maintenance calls received during a defined period and linked to a property or unit identifier. Exclude tests, duplicates, and calls with no recoverable request; publish the exclusion counts. Record reported issue, location, access preference, safety trigger, rule-based priority, destination, acknowledgment time, and resolution state. Keep the time zone and property operating hours explicit.

Separate “reported emergency,” “rule-classified urgent,” “routine,” and “unknown.” The first is a caller statement. The second is an application of the owner’s rule. The difference is analytically important because it shows where rules and language diverge.

## Measures

Measure issue capture, required-field completeness, priority-rule agreement, owner acknowledgment, and unresolved aging. Use counts and defined denominators. A priority-rule agreement rate should be based on records with enough information to apply the rule. Do not count a missing field as a correct routine classification.

Analyze by property, issue type, time band, and access constraint if the sample permits. A building with older systems may have a different issue mix from a newer property. Cross-property comparisons require comparable rules and documented differences.

## Safety and privacy

When the caller reports fire, gas, flooding, exposed electrical danger, violence, or medical distress, use the owner’s emergency path and avoid delaying urgent help for a complete form. Do not give technical instructions outside the approved script. Retain only details needed for response. NIST’s Privacy Framework emphasizes governance and data processing risk; a property manager still must set the actual retention rule.

Residents may disclose health, family, or financial details while explaining access. Do not repeat those details in a general maintenance note unless necessary and authorized. Use an access-preference field rather than a narrative about a resident’s circumstances.

## Review

Reconcile a sample of phone records, work orders, and owner acknowledgment. Check whether the same request received multiple identifiers, whether priority changed after inspection, and whether the change was documented as a later fact. Later inspection can refine a work order but cannot rewrite what the caller reported.

Ask two reviewers to code priority against the published rule. Low agreement indicates ambiguity. High agreement does not prove the rule is fair or legally sufficient; it only shows that reviewers applied the text similarly.

## Interpretation

Many “urgent” reports with routine classification may indicate a communication problem or an overbroad caller label. Many routine reports later escalated may indicate missing questions, not caller unreliability. Track both directions. A faster acknowledgment may be beneficial, but it does not prove faster repair or resident satisfaction.

## Limitations

Property rules differ, contractor availability changes, and the phone record cannot observe the physical condition. Resolution timestamps may be delayed or entered in batches. Legal requirements vary by location. The study is operational evidence, not a habitability opinion.

## Data dictionary

Define a maintenance request, duplicate, acknowledgment, inspection, and resolution before coding. A request can remain open after an initial visit. An acknowledgment can mean that the owner accepted the work order, not that the repair was complete. Keep those states separate. If a resident calls again about the same issue, link the events when the property system permits it and preserve the repeat-call count.

## Fairness and access

Review whether access options are offered consistently across residents and locations. A request for a language accommodation, relay support, or a different entry window is an operational field, not a reason to lower priority. When a resident cannot provide a requested detail, record the gap and identify who can resolve it. Do not infer intent from incomplete communication.

## Change control

Property managers should date every priority-rule change and state which issue types it affects. Recalculate historical data only when the report clearly labels the revised classification. Otherwise, compare like with like. A rule that produces high reviewer agreement may still need owner or legal review if it omits a recognized exception.

## Reporting

Show the queue by reported issue, applied priority, owner acknowledgment, and unresolved age. Include the number of records whose condition changed after inspection. That makes the difference between intake evidence and physical evidence visible and prevents a phone record from being treated as a technical inspection.

## Conclusion

Property-maintenance research should make priority traceable: report what the resident said, show the rule applied, identify the owner, and preserve later changes. This separation helps VirtualAssistantCallCenter’s audience improve routing while respecting technical, legal, privacy, and emergency boundaries.
